Two regulatory regimes hit cashmere imports at the same time in 2026, and most B2B buyers we talk to in Q3 are not fully prepared for either. CBAM (the EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism) finished its transitional phase at the end of 2025 and is now in active enforcement for many imported textile categories. UFLPA (the US Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act) has been active since 2022 but the practical documentation burden on apparel importers has materially increased in 2026 as CBP tightened enforcement on small lots that previously slipped through.

This page is a buyer's checklist. We update it quarterly based on what customs actually holds, not what consultants write. If you are seeing something different in your market, we want to hear it — the comment form is open.

## 1. The two regimes in plain terms

**EU CBAM (Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism).** Carbon levy on imported goods based on the emissions embedded in their production. For cashmere, the relevant emissions are (a) electricity used in dehairing, washing, spinning, and (b) process heat for drying. The EU began CBAM in transitional phase on 1 Oct 2023 with reporting only. From 1 Jan 2026, CBAM certificates must be purchased by importers, calculated on declared embedded emissions. The first certificate purchase is due in 2026 (for 2025 imports) — meaning the cashmere lots you are buying now in Q4 2026 will be the first ones subject to actual levy.

**US UFLPA / WRO (Withhold Release Orders).** Under UFLPA, any goods mined, produced, or manufactured wholly or in part in Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region are prohibited from entry into the US unless the importer can demonstrate by clear and convincing evidence that the goods were not produced with forced labor. CBP issues commodity-specific WROs that expand over time. Apparel and textiles — including cashmere — are a high-priority category. The practical effect for cashmere importers: every shipment must have a documented chain of custody from the herding cooperative to the finished garment, with verifiable origin.

**Lacey Act (US).** Independent of UFLPA, the Lacey Act prohibits trade in illegally sourced plant and animal products. For cashmere, this means you must be able to document that the fiber was not poached from wild goats (rare, but happens) and that any post-2008 import complied with foreign and US permitting requirements.

## 2. The minimum paperwork we provide to wholesale clients

This is the documentation set that gets EU and US cashmere shipments through customs with the fewest holds. We provide it as a stamped PDF on company letterhead for every wholesale PO:

- **Certificate of Origin** — issued by our local Industry & Commerce Bureau (鄂尔多斯市工商行政管理局), stating the place of dehairing, spinning, and finishing. Not a self-issued factory certificate.
- **Processing flow declaration** — describing each manufacturing step and the geographic location where it occurs, from raw fiber receipt to finished yarn or garment. This is what UFLPA enforcement specifically looks for.
- **Traceback summary** — for the fiber lots in your shipment, identifying the herder cooperatives (合作社) that supplied the raw cashmere fiber, with their approximate locations and the season of harvest. Inner Mongolia outside of Xinjiang = no UFLPA issue, but CBP wants the paperwork anyway.
- **Electricity source declaration** — required for CBAM. Our facility is on Inner Mongolia grid, which is approximately 65% coal / 35% wind and solar. We provide the Chinese grid emission factor and the verifiable kWh consumption per kg of yarn output. This is what determines your CBAM certificate cost.
- **Fur-fiber absence declaration** — for shipments to the EU and California, confirming that no angora or rabbit fiber is blended into the lot. Not strictly required, but a routine question from CBP on small cashmere lots.
- **OEKO-TEX Standard 100 certificate** — already on file for our yarns, downloadable from the buyer dashboard. Reduces the chemical-input questions that CBP can raise under UFLPA.

## 3. The customs holds we have actually seen in 2026

In Q1–Q3 2026, our EU and US clients have reported the following customs-side issues, in order of frequency. None of them are unsolvable but each of them adds 2–6 weeks of supply-chain delay if paperwork is not preemptively in order.

**Most common (about 70% of holds):** CBP or EU customs requesting a more detailed processing-flow declaration than the standard one. Usually fixed by providing a mill-level breakdown with line numbers, machine types, and kWh per process. Cost: 1–2 weeks of delay.

**Second most common (about 20%):** CBAM-related questions on a small lot (under €10,000) where the importer assumed CBAM did not yet apply to textiles. CBAM does apply; the levies are low for small lots but the importer still needs to file. Cost: 3–4 weeks of delay for first-time filers.

**Rare (about 10%):** UFLPA full chain-of-custody review on a shipment that the importer could not tie back to a specific herder cooperative. Fixed by our traceback documentation. Cost: 4–6 weeks of delay.

The pattern: most holds come from importer-side paperwork gaps, not factory-side issues. If you are a brand importing cashmere and you have not yet built a relationship with a customs broker who handles fiber/textile categories, that is the single highest-leverage step you can take in 2026.

## 4. The 2026 cashmere-specific test budget

For routine shipments under $50K, you do not need to budget for testing. The Certificate of Origin + processing flow + OEKO-TEX should be enough.

For larger shipments ($50K–$500K), budget for:
- **Fiber composition verification** by an ISO 17025 lab: ~$200–$400 per lot, recommended for first-time orders and new supplier onboarding
- **Micron testing** (laser-based): confirms the 14–15.5µm claim. ~$150–$300 per lot
- **OEKO-TEX certificate verification** (if shipping under your own certificate rather than ours): ~$500–$1,500 annual depending on scope

For shipments over $500K (department-store-level contracts), most buyers in 2026 are doing:
- All of the above
- Plus an **independent social compliance audit** of the factory, even if UFLPA does not strictly require it. Several EU retailers are making this a procurement requirement regardless of regulatory exposure. We work with two audit firms; both can deliver a 1-day on-site visit in our facility within 4–6 weeks of request.

## 5. The Inner Mongolia origin question

A clarification we get often: **Inner Mongolia (内蒙古) is a separate region from Xinjiang (新疆)**, and they are not subject to the same enforcement. All of our dehairing, spinning, and finishing occurs at our facility in the Ordos Industrial Park, Otog Front Banner, Ordos City, Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region. Fiber sourcing is from cooperatives in Alxa League, Xilingol League, and Ordos — all within Inner Mongolia. None of our fiber, yarn, or finished goods transit Xinjiang.

This is straightforward to verify on a Customs form — Inner Mongolia is its own customs zone and the document stamp will reflect that. The holds we have seen in 2026 have been paperwork-completeness issues, not origin disputes. If you are working with a different supplier whose documentation traces to Xinjiang, that is a separate conversation.

## 6. What to ask your supplier before placing the order

A 6-question checklist you can send any cashmere supplier in 2026:

1. Can you issue a Certificate of Origin on company letterhead with the Industry & Commerce Bureau stamp?
2. Can you provide a per-process flow declaration identifying each step, machine type, and electricity consumption?
3. Can you identify the herder cooperatives that supply the fiber, with locations and season?
4. Is your facility in Inner Mongolia, Ningxia, or another region outside of Xinjiang? (If Xinjiang, walk away — UFLPA exposure is total.)
5. Do you have an OEKO-TEX Standard 100 certificate? (Most reputable mills do.)
6. Have any of your 2025–2026 shipments experienced a customs hold? (A "yes" with documented resolution is fine. A "no, never" from a supplier with 100+ shipments is statistically unlikely and may indicate they're not paying attention.)

If your supplier cannot answer all six, the customs hold risk is on you, not on them.
