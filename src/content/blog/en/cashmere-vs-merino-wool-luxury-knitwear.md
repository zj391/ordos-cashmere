# Cashmere vs Merino: Why Luxury Brands Pay 8x More (And When Merino Makes More Sense)

Cashmere averages 14.5-16μm at 30-80mm staple length. Merino averages 18-24μm at 70-120mm. That gap explains most of the price difference — but not all of it, and it is not the whole reason a luxury brand specifies cashmere.

This is a mill-side comparison: where cashmere genuinely wins, where merino is the better engineering choice, the cases where a blend beats both pure, and what to write into a spec to get the fiber you actually meant.

## The numbers behind the price

| Property | Cashmere | Merino | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Average fiber diameter | 14.5-16μm | 18-24μm | Below 16μm the fiber bends rather than pricks. Above 19μm you feel the barb tips. |
| Staple length | 30-80mm | 70-120mm | Long merino needs less twist; short cashmere needs more twist to stay together. |
| Down content | 85-98% | 70-90% | Cashmere's near-total absence of guard hair is why it feels smooth rather than waxy. |
| Scale structure | unique, memory-like | conventional wool scales | Cashmere's crimp returns to its coiled state after stretching. That is the source of cashmere's "recovery". |
| Raw-to-sorted yield | 1 kg raw → 0.30-0.40 kg sorted | 1 kg raw → 0.60-0.70 kg sorted | The yield gap alone explains a 2-3x price difference. |
| Micron premium | 14.5μm vs 16.5μm = 1.6-2.1x price | not graded as tightly | Luxury cashmere is graded tight; merino rarely is. |

**The price is mostly yield plus sorting, not a marketing artifact.** A kilo of 15.5μm sorted cashmere requires roughly 3 kg of raw fiber, and most of that raw fiber is not 15.5μm — it gets rejected and goes to a coarser count program or a blend line. Merino at 20μm is accepted straight from the scouring stage at 65% yield.

## Where cashmere genuinely wins

**1. The 19-micron threshold.** This is the real dividing line, not the brand. Above roughly 19μm, merino fibers are the same diameter as ordinary wool and the barb tips start to register as prickle. Below it, cashmere's fine diameter is genuinely a different tactile experience. A buyer whose target customer complains about itching is describing a micron problem, not a fiber-identity problem.

**2. Recovery after stretching.** Cashmere's crimp is not a permanent deformation — pull a cashmere sweater and it returns to shape because the fiber's scale structure lets it. Merino has excellent recovery too, but a lower plateau: repeated stretching of a merino garment flattens the knit structure faster because the fiber itself is coarser and stiffer.

**3. Down content and smoothness.** 95% down cashmere has almost no coarse fiber in the blend. Merino's down content is typically 75-85%, so 15-25% of the fiber is guard hair — which is what makes merino slightly prickly when worn next to the skin and what makes it itch more after washing.

**4. Low-density drape.** Cashmere's specific gravity is lower than wool at the same diameter. A cashmere fabric of the same GSM drapes closer to the body; merino of the same GSM stands slightly away. This is why a lightweight cashmere scarf folds and a lightweight merino scarf does not.

## Where merino is the better engineering choice

**1. Durability.** Merino fibers are longer (70-120mm vs 30-80mm) and stronger at the same diameter. For a blanket, a throw, a coat, or anything a customer will wash at home, merino outlasts cashmere by a factor that shows up in return rates.

**2. Machine washability.** Cashmere felts and shrinks if a customer washes it wrong, and a cashmere product has to be sold with a "dry clean only" instruction. Merino can be machine washed on a wool cycle at low temperature and comes out wearable. For a brand selling to customers who do not read care labels, that is a product decision worth more than fiber prestige.

**3. Cost ceiling on large-format goods.** A 200×200cm blanket in 100% cashmere is expensive enough that the price becomes the objection. The same blanket in merino, or in a 70/30 cashmere-merino blend, lands in a range where the customer compares it to other blankets rather than to other cashmere.

**4. Colour depth on deep shades.** Cashmere takes dye beautifully but, because the fiber is so fine, deep shades (navy, black, burgundy) can read flatter on cashmere than on merino — the light scatter off more surface fibers. For a brand whose hero colour is a saturated dark, this is a real consideration.

## The four cases where a blend beats both pure

**1. 70/30 cashmere-merino for mid-weight knitwear.** The merino adds the length and strength the cashmere lacks; the cashmere adds the softness and drape. This is the most common blend in premium knitwear for good structural reasons, not cost.

**2. 85/15 cashmere-silk for fluid drape.** Silk adds a slipperiness that cashmere alone cannot reach, and it takes dye to a depth that cashmere cannot. Used for scarves and lightweight tops. The tradeoff: silk reduces recovery and pilling resistance.

**3. 80/20 cashmere-nylon for abrasion resistance.** Not a luxury hand, but it extends garment life dramatically. Used where the garment will be worn hard — travel knitwear, not evening pieces.

**4. 70/30 cashmere-wool for outerwear.** The wool adds body and wind resistance. The cashmere keeps the next-to-skin surface soft. Standard for cardigans and coats that need to hold a shape.

## What to write into a spec so you get the fiber you meant

The most common sourcing error we see is a spec that says "cashmere" and nothing else, which lets a mill ship a 19.5μm coarser count that is technically cashmere and functionally not what the brand had in mind.

A working spec:

> 100% cashmere. Average fiber diameter 15.5μm ±0.5μm per ISO 18135 on a 30,000-fiber sample. Down content 93% minimum per GB/T 25262. Staple length 38-55mm mean. Guard hair content below 3% by weight.

The two clauses that do the work are the **micron band with a named test method** and the **staple length band**. Diameter alone lets a mill argue about test method; diameter plus length plus down content does not leave room.

## The mill-side view

At our Ordos facility we run cashmere across the full range — 14.5μm for luxury hand, 16.5μm for the volume commercial lines, and 19.5μm for blends and utility applications. The micron choice is not a quality ladder; it is a design decision. A brand that specifies 16.5μm for a scarf and 14.5μm for a next-to-skin layer is making a better product than one that specifies 15.5μm for both.

The buyers who get the best result are the ones who tell us the garment, the target retail price, and the customer — not just the micron. From those three we can tell you whether cashmere is the right fiber at all, or whether merino or a blend serves the product better.
