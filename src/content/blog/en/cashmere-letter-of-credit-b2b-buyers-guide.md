# Cashmere Letter of Credit Payment: A B2B Buyer's Complete 2026 Guide

If you're placing your first USD 30,000+ cashmere order with a Chinese factory, the payment conversation almost always lands on an **irrevocable Letter of Credit at sight (L/C)**. It protects both sides: the factory gets a bank-guaranteed payment once shipment documents match the L/C terms, and you keep payment leverage until the goods actually leave the port. But L/Cs are also where first-time importers lose money — a mismatched document set, a missing inspection certificate, or a clause the factory can't meet will stop the bank from paying and stall your cargo.

This guide explains how L/Cs work for cashmere specifically, what documents our Ordos facility routinely issues, which clauses B2B buyers should require, and what an L/C typically costs you in fees. We've processed USD 11M+ in L/C transactions over the last three years across 38 countries, so the examples below are real cases, not theory.

## What an L/C Actually Does

A Letter of Credit is a bank instrument: your issuing bank (e.g., HSBC, Deutsche Bank, Bank of America) promises to pay the beneficiary (us, the factory) once we present a set of documents that exactly match the terms you specified. The bank doesn't inspect the goods — it inspects the documents. If our commercial invoice says 1,200 kg of 2/26 Nm cashmere yarn and the packing list says 1,200 kg, the bank pays. If one document says 1,200 kg and another says 1,250 kg, the bank refuses and you have a problem.

There are three flavors B2B cashmere buyers typically use:

- **Irrevocable L/C at sight** — bank pays within 7 days of receiving conforming documents. Most common for new B2B relationships.
- **L/C with deferred payment (usance)** — bank pays 30/60/90 days after sight. Useful if you want to receive goods and sell them before paying. Slightly higher fee.
- **Confirmed L/C** — a second bank in the seller's country (often HSBC China or Bank of China) adds its guarantee on top of your issuing bank. Required if your issuing bank is unknown to the Chinese factory. Adds 1-2% fee.

For first-time orders of USD 30K-150K, the **irrevocable L/C at sight** is the standard default at our factory. Established buyers (3+ orders, USD 100K+ annual spend) can move to T/T 30/70 or OA 30 days after we have payment history.

## Required Documents for a Cashmere L/C

Our Ordos factory issues the following documents for every L/C shipment. Your L/C terms should explicitly require each one:

1. **Signed Commercial Invoice** — 3 originals + 3 copies. States product description (e.g., "100% cashmere yarn, 2/26 Nm, lot number, net weight"), unit price, total value, Incoterm, payment terms.
2. **Full set (3/3) of clean-on-board Bills of Lading** — made out "to order of [your issuing bank]", marked "freight prepaid" (for CIF) or "freight collect" (for FOB).
3. **Packing List** — net/gross weight per carton, dimensions, carton count, HS code (5108.20 for yarn, 6110.20 for knitwear, 6202 for coats).
4. **Certificate of Origin** — issued by China Council for the Promotion of International Trade (CCPIT). Free for our shipments, processed in 1-2 working days.
5. **Quality Certificate / Inspection Report** — internal QC report for raw material and finished goods. For buyers requiring pre-shipment inspection (PSI), we add the SGS/Bureau Veritas/Intertek certificate here.
6. **Insurance Certificate** (for CIF/CIP shipments) — covers 110% of CIF value, ICC A clauses.
7. **Beneficiary's Certificate** — one-page letter confirming shipment was made per L/C terms. Standard clause.

Documents 1-4 are non-negotiable; documents 5-7 depend on your L/C wording and the Incoterm you've chosen. Mismatches on document 1, 3, or 4 cause 80% of L/C discrepancies — that's where B2B buyers should focus their review.

## Five Clauses That Protect B2B Buyers

A well-drafted L/C for cashmere should include these clauses:

**1. Partial shipments allowed.** Cashmere yarn is heavy (1,200 kg in our example) and may ship in two containers if the production line finishes the dye lots in stages. Forbidding partial shipments forces the factory to hold the entire order until the last lot is ready, which can add 2-4 weeks to lead time.

**2. Transshipment allowed.** Yarn and knitwear often route through Singapore or Busan before reaching the US/EU. If your L/C forbids transshipment, the goods can't load on a vessel that calls at an intermediate port — limiting shipping options and raising freight cost 15-25%.

**3. 21-day presentation period after B/L date.** Banks give sellers 21 days from the Bill of Lading date to present documents. A 14-day period is too tight — if customs clearance in Tianjin takes a week, we can't meet the document deadline. The UCP 600 default is 21 days; keep it that way.

**4. Tolerance of ±5% on quantity and value.** Yarn weight can shift slightly during dehairing (fiber loss) and knitting (yarn recovery). A 0% tolerance forces the factory to re-weigh and re-issue documents if the actual shipment is 1,195 kg instead of 1,200 kg. The standard ±5% tolerance per UCP 600 avoids this entire class of discrepancy.

**5. HS code 5108.20 (yarn) / 6110.20 (knitwear) / 6202 (coats).** Match your commercial invoice exactly to the L/C wording. If the L/C says 5108 but the invoice says 5108.20, that's a discrepancy. Be specific in your L/C draft.

These five clauses appear in roughly 90% of the L/Cs we receive from European and American buyers. If yours doesn't have them, your issuing bank can still process the L/C — but you're creating friction that may delay payment by 7-14 days if a discrepancy arises.

## Fees B2B Buyers Should Budget

L/C fees stack up on both sides. For a USD 50,000 L/C, here's the rough breakdown:

- **Issuing bank fee (buyer side)**: 0.125-0.25% of L/C value, typically USD 100-150 minimum. Some banks charge a flat USD 200-300 for small L/Cs.
- **Advising bank fee (factory side)**: USD 50-150 flat in China. The factory typically absorbs this.
- **Confirmation fee** (if confirmed L/C): 1-2% of L/C value, USD 500-1,000 on a USD 50K L/C. Only needed if your issuing bank is unknown to Chinese banks.
- **Document handling fee**: USD 50-200 per set of documents presented.
- **Discrepancy fee**: USD 50-100 per discrepancy. If documents don't match L/C terms exactly.

For a clean USD 50K L/C at sight without confirmation, total buyer-side fees are typically **USD 200-500**. That's roughly 0.5-1% of order value — much cheaper than a wire transfer fraud or a customs dispute, but real money you should budget.

## Common L/C Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

Three mistakes account for most L/C discrepancies in our experience:

**Mismatch between L/C and PO.** The purchase order says "cashmere sweater 14.5μm 2/26 Nm" but the L/C says "cashmere pullover 14-16μm 2/28 Nm". The micron range and yarn count differ. Bank refuses documents. Fix: copy the L/C wording verbatim from your PO before sending to the issuing bank. Have the factory review the L/C draft before issuance — we offer this as a free service for new buyers.

**Late shipment date.** L/Cs have a latest shipment date (typically 30-45 days after issuance). If production is delayed by even a day, the L/C expires. Fix: build a 10-day buffer into your shipment date, or include a 15-day grace period clause.

**Missing inspection certificate.** Some L/Cs require SGS or Bureau Veritas pre-shipment inspection (PSI), but the factory doesn't realize this and ships before booking the inspection. Fix: explicitly confirm with the factory that PSI is required, and book the inspection 5-7 days before shipment.

If a discrepancy does occur, you have three options: (1) ask the factory to fix and re-present documents (delays payment 7-14 days), (2) accept the discrepancy and pay (you'll be charged the discrepancy fee), or (3) negotiate with the factory for a discount to cover the discrepancy fee.

## When L/C Isn't the Right Choice

L/Cs are great for risk management on first orders and large contracts, but they have costs that don't fit every transaction:

- **Small trial orders (under USD 10,000)**: L/C fees eat 5-10% of order value. Use T/T 30/70 or PayPal for trial orders.
- **Repeat orders with established factories**: T/T 30/70 is faster (1 day to clear vs 7-14 for L/C) and cheaper.
- **Buyers with weak banking relationships**: If your issuing bank is small or unfamiliar with Asian trade, you'll be quoted high fees or refused L/C issuance. Work with HSBC, Citi, JPMorgan, Deutsche Bank, or Standard Chartered for best terms.

For our existing buyers, the typical payment ladder is: T/T 30/70 for the first USD 30K trial order → L/C at sight for USD 30K-150K production orders → OA 30 days once you hit USD 100K+ annual spend. That's the path most of our 500+ active B2B clients have followed.
