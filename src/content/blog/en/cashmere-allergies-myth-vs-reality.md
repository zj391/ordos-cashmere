# Cashmere Allergies: Myth, Reality, and What to Tell Your Customers

As a B2B buyer or brand owner, you’ve likely encountered customers who claim they are “allergic to cashmere.” This concern can be a barrier to sales, but it’s often based on a misunderstanding of fiber science. In this article, we’ll separate fact from fiction, explain why cashmere is generally well-tolerated by sensitive skin, and give you the facts you need to address customer concerns confidently.

## The Myth: “I’m Allergic to Cashmere”

True allergies to cashmere are extremely rare. Cashmere is a natural protein fiber derived from the undercoat of cashmere goats. Unlike synthetic fibers or certain wools, cashmere fibers are fine (typically 14–19 microns) and smooth, which reduces the mechanical irritation that triggers itchiness.

Most people who say they are “allergic” to cashmere are actually experiencing a reaction to:

- **Coarse fibers** in lower-grade cashmere or blends (e.g., with sheep’s wool)
- **Chemical residues** from dyes, bleaches, or finishing agents
- **Lanolin** – a natural oil present in sheep’s wool, but NOT in cashmere (cashmere goats do not produce lanolin)
- **Friction and heat** – tight weaves or high humidity can cause sweating and irritation

The key point: Cashmere is not inherently allergenic. In fact, it is often recommended for people with sensitive skin, eczema, or wool allergies.

## The Science: Why Cashmere is Gentle on Skin

Cashmere’s hypoallergenic properties stem from its physical structure:

- **Fine diameter**: The average cashmere fiber is 14–19 microns, compared to merino wool at 20–24 microns and regular wool at 30+ microns. Finer fibers bend more easily, so they don’t prick the skin.
- **Smooth surface**: Cashmere fibers have a smooth, scaly surface with less scale protrusion than wool, reducing friction and irritation.
- **No lanolin**: Lanolin is a common allergen found in sheep’s wool. Cashmere goats do not produce lanolin, making cashmere naturally lanolin-free.
- **Breathability and moisture management**: Cashmere can absorb up to 30% of its weight in moisture without feeling damp, helping to regulate skin temperature and reduce sweat-induced irritation.

As you can see, cashmere is objectively one of the most skin-friendly natural fibers available.

## The Reality: What Can Cause Reactions?

While true cashmere allergies are rare, some customers may experience discomfort. Here are the real culprits:

### 1. Poor Quality or Blended Fibers

Low-grade cashmere (e.g., 30mm fiber length or less) or blends containing sheep’s wool or synthetic fibers can cause itching. Always check the fiber composition label – a 100% cashmere garment should not contain wool.

### 2. Chemical Residues

Non-certified dyes and finishing agents can trigger contact dermatitis. To avoid this, source cashmere that is OEKO-TEX® Standard 100 certified, which ensures the absence of harmful substances.

### 3. Individual Sensitivities

Some people have particularly reactive skin and may feel a slight prickling sensation even with fine fibers. This is not an allergy but a sensory response.

### 4. Misdiagnosis

Many people confuse a reaction to synthetic blends (e.g., acrylic) with cashmere. Always verify the actual garment content.

## What to Tell Your Customers

As a B2B partner, you can educate your retail customers and end consumers with these key messages:

- **Cashmere is hypoallergenic** – it lacks lanolin and has fine, smooth fibers that minimize irritation.
- **Quality matters** – invest in high-grade cashmere (14–16 microns) from reputable sources to ensure a premium, non-irritating product.
- **Wash before wearing** – washing removes any residual processing chemicals. Use a gentle detergent designed for wool/cashmere.
- **Layer properly** – cashmere should be worn over a thin cotton base if the wearer has extremely sensitive skin.
- **Check for blends** – if a product is blended with wool or synthetic fibers, that may be the cause of irritation, not the cashmere itself.

## How to Handle Customer Complaints

If a customer reports a reaction to a cashmere garment, follow this checklist:

1. **Ask for details** – What exactly did they wear? Was it 100% cashmere? What was the washing method?
2. **Check the label** – Look for fiber composition and care instructions.
3. **Recommend a patch test** – Suggest testing a small area on the inner arm before wearing.
4. **Offer a solution** – If the garment is defective, replace it. If it’s a blend, offer a 100% cashmere alternative.
5. **Educate** – Provide care instructions and explain why cashmere is generally safe.
