# Cashmere Custom Dye Workflow: From Pantone to Finished Yarn (B2B OEM Guide)

Custom dyeing is where most cashmere OEM projects either succeed brilliantly or fail expensively. Get the dye process right, and your brand's signature camel coat, signature navy crewneck, or signature off-white scarf sells for years. Get it wrong — wrong Pantone match, poor color fastness, batch-to-batch variation — and you ship product that customers return. This guide walks through the end-to-end custom dye workflow from Pantone code to finished yarn, with specific numbers on lead times, MOQ, cost premiums, and the most common failure modes B2B buyers should plan around.

Most cashmere custom dye work happens at a small number of specialized dye houses in Inner Mongolia, Hebei, and Zhejiang. Our facility partners with two dye houses (one in Ordos, one in Langfang) for 90% of our custom color work. Both follow similar workflows with minor variations in equipment. The process below reflects what you should expect from any serious cashmere dye house, whether you're ordering 100 kg or 10,000 kg.

## Why Custom Dyeing Matters for B2B Cashmere Brands

Stock colors (white, navy, gray, camel, black) cover 60-70% of cashmere production globally. The other 30-40% is custom color work — brands building a signature palette, capsule collections, or color-coordinated sets that require exact Pantone matches. The custom color premium is real but manageable:

- **Stock color yarn**: USD 60-90 per kg (depending on yarn count, micron, ply)
- **Custom color yarn**: USD 75-120 per kg (USD 15-30/kg premium for the dye process, lab dips, smaller dye lots)

For a typical sweater using 250 g of yarn, that's USD 4-8 extra per garment. For a USD 80 retail sweater, that's 5-10% margin impact — meaningful but rarely a deal-breaker.

The bigger consideration is lead time. Custom dye adds 2-4 weeks to your production schedule (vs. stock yarn) and requires earlier commitment to colors. For seasonal collection drops (SS27 in February, AW27 in September), you'll need your Pantone palette finalized 6 months ahead. For stock programs (year-round basics in 5-7 stock colors), you can order any time and skip the custom workflow entirely.

## Step 1: Pantone Submission and Color Specification

The custom dye process starts with a Pantone code. Most B2B buyers specify colors using **Pantone Textile Cotton (TCX)** or **Pantone Textile Paper (TPG)** systems, which are calibrated for textile applications. RGB or CMYK from screen designs don't translate cleanly to dye recipes — always use a physical Pantone swatch book.

**What you submit to the dye house**:

- Pantone TCX/TPG code (e.g., Pantone 17-1230 TCX "Mocha Mousse" for 2025's Pantone Color of the Year)
- Yarn specification: count (2/26 Nm, 2/48 Nm), micron (14.5μm, 16μm), ply (1-ply, 2-ply)
- Quantity per color (minimum 5 kg per color for most dye houses)
- Application: yarn-dyed (pre-knit) vs. garment-dyed (post-knit)
- Target delivery date

**Common pitfalls**:
- Submitting a Pantone from the wrong system (TPX vs. TCX). Pantone revised several thousand codes in 2023 when TPX transitioned to TPG — make sure you have a current TPG/TCX book.
- Specifying a metallic or fluorescent Pantone that can't be achieved with cashmere fiber. Some Pantones require synthetic fibers or special treatments.
- Asking for a Pantone that's outside the standard textile gamut. Pantone Solid Coated has a wider gamut than Pantone TCX, which is calibrated for textiles. Colors outside the textile gamut require approximation and won't match exactly.

The dye house typically responds within 1-3 days confirming feasibility and quoting the lab dip fee.

## Step 2: Lab Dip and Color Approval

A lab dip is a 50-100 g sample of yarn dyed to your specified Pantone using the actual dye recipe the dye house will use for bulk production. Most serious dye houses produce lab dips within 5-10 days.

**The lab dip process**:

- **Recipe formulation**: Master dyer combines reactive dyes, acid dyes, or a blend (depending on fiber content and target color). For 100% cashmere, acid dyes are standard.
- **Sample dyeing**: 50-100 g of yarn in a small laboratory dyeing machine.
- **Color measurement**: Spectrophotometer measures the lab dip against your target Pantone. The color difference (ΔE) is reported. **ΔE < 1.0** is excellent match, **ΔE 1.0-2.0** is acceptable for most applications, **ΔE > 2.5** is visibly different.
- **Physical sample**: The dye house ships the lab dip yarn to you (or shares a high-resolution photo with spectrophotometer data). Most B2B buyers require a physical sample, not just a digital reading.

**Lab dip costs**: USD 50-150 per color per attempt. Most Pantones can be matched in 1-2 attempts; tricky colors (pastels, fluorescent-adjacent brights, very dark blues) may take 3-4 attempts. Budget USD 200-500 total for the lab dip phase per Pantone.

**Critical: never skip this step**. We learned this the hard way in 2017 when a buyer approved a lab dip based on a digital photo, only to find the bulk dye lot was visibly different (ΔE 3.5). They rejected the entire 800 kg batch and ate USD 18K in costs. Always receive, touch, and approve a physical lab dip before bulk production.

## Step 3: Bulk Dye Lot Planning

Once you've signed off on the lab dip, the dye house plans the bulk dye lot. Most cashmere dye lots run 50-500 kg per color (dye machine capacity determines the maximum). Smaller lots (<50 kg) trigger per-kg premium fees.

**Bulk dye lot minimums and pricing**:

| Lot size (per color) | Per-kg premium over stock | Typical use case |
|----------------------|---------------------------|------------------|
| 5-25 kg              | USD 25-40/kg premium      | Sample runs, capsule collections |
| 25-100 kg            | USD 15-25/kg premium      | Standard B2B custom orders |
| 100-500 kg           | USD 8-15/kg premium       | Bulk production runs |
| 500-2,000 kg         | USD 5-10/kg premium       | Large brand annual programs |

For a 100-piece sweater run (25 kg yarn at 250 g per sweater), you're paying USD 15-25/kg premium. For a 1,000-piece run (250 kg), USD 8-15/kg premium. The economies of scale are significant.

**Lot planning considerations**:

- **Fiber lot consistency**: Cashmere fiber from the same combing season and region takes dye more uniformly than mixed lots. The dye house typically holds yarn from a single lot for your dye run to ensure color consistency.
- **Color sequence**: If you're dyeing 8 colors, the dye house sequences them from lightest to darkest to minimize machine cleaning between colors. Black to white requires 3-4 hours of machine cleaning; white to black requires 30 minutes.
- **Reserve buffer**: Order 5-10% more yarn than your immediate production need. Yarn-dye waste (yarn that breaks during knitting, ends that get discarded) typically runs 3-7%. Plus reserve gives you headroom for color matching if a batch needs to be redyed.

## Step 4: Bulk Dyeing and Quality Testing

Bulk dye runs take 3-7 days depending on lot size and color complexity. Light colors (pastels, off-whites) often take longer than dark colors because they're more sensitive to fiber impurities.

**Bulk dye process**:

- **Pre-treatment**: Yarn is scoured (washed) to remove fiber oils, dirt, and processing residues. Pre-treatment quality directly affects dye uptake.
- **Dyeing**: Yarn is loaded into the dye machine (typically a side-paddle or jet dyeing machine). Dye liquor circulates at controlled temperature (80-100°C for acid dyes on cashmere). Dye cycle runs 60-120 minutes.
- **Rinse and soften**: After dyeing, yarn is rinsed multiple times to remove unfixed dye, then softened with a cationic softener. Softener improves yarn hand-feel.
- **Drying**: Hydro-extraction (centrifuge) followed by radio-frequency or hot-air drying. Yarn moisture content is brought to 8-12% for storage and knitting.
- **Winding**: Finished yarn is wound onto cones or into hanks, depending on your knitting machine's input format.

**Quality testing during bulk production**:

- **Color measurement**: Spectrophotometer checks bulk lot color against the approved lab dip. ΔE should be within 0.5 of lab dip — anything wider means the dye recipe needs adjustment.
- **Color fastness testing**:
  - **Wash fastness** (ISO 105-C06): Tests color loss and bleeding during washing. Acceptable: 4+ on grey scale (1-5 scale, higher is better).
  - **Light fastness** (ISO 105-B02): Tests color fading under UV exposure. Acceptable: 4+ for most uses, 5+ for outdoor applications.
  - **Rub fastness** (ISO 105-X12): Tests color transfer when rubbed. Acceptable: 4+ dry, 3+ wet.
- **Hand-feel check**: Dyed yarn hand-feel should match the undyed base yarn. Excessive dye infiltration can roughen the fiber.
- **Strength check**: Drawn yarn tensile strength should be within 10% of the undyed base. Over-dyeing can degrade fiber.

The dye house typically runs these tests on every bulk lot and provides a certificate of compliance. Serious B2B buyers (especially EU/US retail) require this certificate before accepting delivery.

## Step 5: Delivery and Color Approval

Bulk dye lots typically deliver 4-6 weeks after lab dip approval. Larger lots (500+ kg per color) may take 6-8 weeks. The yarn ships on cones (typically 1 kg per cone) with each lot labeled with:

- Pantone code
- Lab dip approval date
- Bulk dye batch number
- Color fastness results
- Net weight
- Dye house name and contact

**Receiving acceptance**:
- **Visual color check**: Compare bulk lot to approved lab dip under standard lighting (D65, the international daylight standard). Color match within ΔE 0.5 = approved. Anything visibly different = hold for investigation.
- **Hand-feel check**: Yarn should feel soft and supple. Harsh or boardy yarn may have been over-processed.
- **Cone integrity**: Yarn should be evenly wound with no breaks or tangles.

Most custom dye orders have a 95%+ first-batch acceptance rate if the lab dip was properly approved. Issues typically arise from rushed lab dip approvals (buyers signing off on digital photos) or production pressure pushing the dye house to skip pre-treatment steps.

## Common Failure Modes and How to Avoid Them

Three problems account for 80% of custom dye issues:

**1. ΔE mismatch between lab dip and bulk lot**

Cause: Dye recipe scale-up from 100 g lab to 100 kg bulk doesn't perfectly reproduce. Water-to-yarn ratio, agitation speed, temperature ramp rates all change.

Fix: Always include a 5-10% buffer in your order. If the first bulk lot doesn't match the lab dip, you have material for a re-dye. If it does match, you have buffer for knitting waste.

**2. Color fastness failures**

Cause: Insufficient dye fixation, inadequate rinsing, or wrong dye class for the fiber.

Fix: Specify required color fastness ratings in your purchase order (4+ wash, 4+ light, 4+ rub). Most serious dye houses meet this without premium pricing. If your brand requires 5+ ratings (e.g., for outdoor or activewear applications), expect a 10-15% price premium and longer dyeing cycles.

**3. Batch-to-batch variation across multiple orders**

Cause: Different dye baths, different fiber lots, different dye house technicians.

Fix: Lock in a single dye house for your entire color palette, even if it costs 5-10% more than splitting work across multiple dye houses. Specify exact dye recipes (the dye house keeps these on file) and request the same dye master for each batch.

For multi-season production (e.g., AW26 + AW27 production of the same color), this consistency is critical. We had one buyer receive visibly different "same Pantone" yarn between AW24 and AW25 because they switched dye houses between seasons to save 8%. They paid USD 12K in customer returns.

## When to Use Yarn-Dyed vs. Garment-Dyed Cashmere

One final consideration: yarn-dyed vs. garment-dyed.

**Yarn-dyed** (the process above): Yarn is dyed before knitting. Each garment uses one color of yarn (or multiple colors for jacquard/intarsia). Better color consistency, more flexible for patterns, slightly higher cost.

**Garment-dyed**: Knitted garment is dyed after construction. Allows multiple colors in one dye lot (e.g., a sweater where body is color A and collar is color B), more efficient for small batch experiments, but limited color consistency between batches.

For most B2B cashmere, yarn-dyed is the standard. Garment-dyed is reserved for special effects (vintage wash, gradient colors, dip-dye) that yarn-dyeing can't achieve.
