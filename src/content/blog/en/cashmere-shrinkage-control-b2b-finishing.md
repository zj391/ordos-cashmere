# Why Your Cashmere Shrinks: 5 Common B2B Manufacturing Mistakes and How to Prevent Them

Cashmere shrinkage is almost always one of five specific process decisions taken at the mill — an under-relaxed yarn, a missing pre-shrink, a wrong dyeing temperature, an aggressive wash cycle, or a wet-finishing step applied after cutting.

Each has a distinct signature in the shrinkage data, which means you can diagnose which one happened rather than just filing a complaint. This covers all five with the mill-side fix for each, the relaxation and pre-shrink treatment that removes most of the risk before the garment is ever cut, and the test method to put in your purchase order.

## Why cashmere shrinks at all

Cashmere has a high natural crimp. That crimp is a stored mechanical energy — the fiber wants to return to a coiled shape. Two processes can release it:

**Thermal process.** Heat plus moisture lets the crimp relax and the fiber contract. Dyeing at 55-60°C with a full bath does this to some degree. If the yarn was never relaxed before dyeing, the relaxation happens *during* dyeing and the skein shrinks onto the bobbin.

**Mechanical process.** Agitation plus moisture. A wash cycle, a wet-finishing pass, a tumble dry — all of them let fibers move and then set in a shorter configuration.

The critical insight: **a relaxed yarn does not shrink later.** An unrelaxed yarn shrinks at whatever point the relaxation energy is released, and if that happens after the garment is cut and shipped, the customer sees it.

## The 5 mistakes, and how each one looks in data

### Mistake 1: Yarn dyed without prior relaxation

**What happened.** The mill skips the relaxation step and dyes the yarn directly. The crimp relaxes in the dye bath, the skein contracts, and the yarn comes off the bobbin shorter than it went on.

**The signature.** Shrinkage is **lengthwise and uniform** — typically 4-8% on every lot, consistent batch to batch. Knit gauge also shifts slightly because the yarn diameter changed.

**The fix.** Specify it in the yarn order:

> Relaxation treatment: steam-relaxed at 95°C for 20 minutes before dyeing. Certificate of relaxation to accompany the delivery.

This is a mill process, not a garment process, and it has to happen before dyeing. Adding it later is not possible.

### Mistake 2: No pre-shrink after dyeing

**What happened.** The yarn was relaxed correctly, but then wet-finished in a way that re-agitated it. The fiber moves and re-sets.

**The signature.** Shrinkage is **lengthwise but lower than mistake 1** — typically 2-5% — and it is more variable lot to lot, because it depends on how hard each particular batch was washed.

**The fix.**

> Post-dye pre-shrink to ISO 6330 method, target residual shrinkage ≤1.5% on a test swatch before cutting.

The pre-shrink is a wash-and-dry step that removes the remaining energy. It costs the mill a wash cycle and a drying cycle and it is very frequently skipped because nobody asked for it.

### Mistake 3: Dye bath temperature out of band

**What happened.** The dye bath ran hot — 65-70°C instead of 55-60°C for cashmere. Higher heat drives more relaxation in the same time.

**The signature.** **Uneven shrinkage between colorways.** A lot that ran at 58°C shrinks 3%; the next lot that ran at 67°C shrinks 7%. When you see lot-to-lot variation larger than about 2%, check the dye house temperature log first.

**The fix.**

> Dye bath temperature 55-60°C, logged per batch, temperature record supplied with the delivery.

### Mistake 4: Aggressive wet finishing or wash

**What happened.** The garment went through a wash cycle set for wool or a heavy fulling process, often because the mill's standard line is set that way and cashmere went through it without a separate setting.

**The signature.** Shrinkage is **area shrinkage in both dimensions** — length and width both reduce, and the width shrinks more than the length because the fabric is under more tension across the width. Often accompanied by a change in hand feel: the fabric gets denser and loses loft.

**The fix.** Ask what the finishing line is set to for cashmere specifically. If the answer is "the same as wool", that is the answer to your question. Specify:

> Cashmere finishing profile: no fulling, gentle wash 20-25 minutes, cold rinse, no tumble heat. Hand-finish only.

### Mistake 5: Wet finishing applied after cutting

**What happened.** The most common sequencing error. The panels are knit, cut, and sewn, and *then* the garment goes through wet finishing. Any shrinkage at that point changes the finished garment dimensions rather than the fabric dimensions.

**The signature.** **The finished garment no longer matches the size chart, but the fabric was fine.** Panels were measured before finishing and were correct; the garment is not. This shows up as systematic size deviation across a whole delivery rather than as random variation.

**The fix.** Sequencing, and it needs to be explicit:

> Wet finishing applied to fabric before cutting and sewing. Bulk cutting only after the finishing pass has been approved on a production-length sample.

## The prevention stack

The five mistakes are independent, but the fix for the first two removes most of the risk. In practice, a yarn that has been steam-relaxed before dyeing and pre-shrunk after dyeing will show under 2% residual shrinkage through normal care, which is inside the range a customer does not notice.

| Step | Removes | Cost to mill |
|---|---|---|
| Steam relaxation before dyeing | Mistake 1 | One steam cycle |
| Post-dye pre-shrink to ISO 6330 | Mistake 2 | One wash + dry cycle |
| Logged dye bath temperature | Mistake 3 | Instrumentation only |
| Cashmere-specific finishing profile | Mistake 4 | Separate setting |
| Finish before cut sequencing | Mistake 5 | Production planning |

Total additional cost is roughly 2-4% of yarn cost, which is well under the cost of one re-delivery.

## The test to put in the purchase order

> **In your spec:** residual shrinkage ≤2% lengthway and ≤2% widthway per ISO 6330 (one wash, 40°C, mild agitation). Test swatch taken from the bulk production fabric, not from a sample cone. Report on the swatch measured from the delivered lot.

The last clause matters. A swatch from a sample cone tells you about the sample. A swatch from the delivered lot tells you about what you are paying for.

## What the customer instruction has to say

Even with all five steps handled, a cashmere garment can shrink if the customer washes it wrong. Two things belong on the care label:

- **Hand wash cold, or dry clean.** Not "machine washable" unless the garment has genuinely been tested for it.
- **Dry flat.** A tumble dryer will shrink any untreated cashmere.

The mills that get the fewest shrinkage complaints are not the ones with the best process alone — they are the ones with the best process *and* an unambiguous care label. A brand that has invested in pre-shrink should say so on the label, because it converts an angry customer into a customer who understands what they bought.

## The mill-side view

At our Ordos facility, relaxation before dyeing and pre-shrink after dyeing are standard on every cashmere line we run, not a premium add-on. We log dye bath temperature per batch and hold wet finishing strictly to a cashmere-specific profile. Our residual shrinkage on bulk production is typically 1-1.5% lengthwise, tested from the delivered lot rather than from a sample.

If you have a current cashmere supplier and you want to know whether their process includes these steps, ask for the relaxation and pre-shrink records for your last three deliveries. A mill that has them will send them within a day. A mill that does not have them will change the subject.
