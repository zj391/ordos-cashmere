This page is a real case study of a 2025-2026 program we ran for a European luxury hotel group — 8,000 pieces of 100% cashmere throw, six colorways, four sizes, two production batches, one mid-program design change, and a final delivery that landed two weeks before the property opening. The hotel is not named in this case study (NDA); the program details are factual and the lessons are general enough to apply to any mid-volume B2B cashmere program.

If you are planning a cashmere program of 3,000-15,000 pieces across 2-6 styles and 2-6 colorways, the program structure, the milestone timing, the QC checkpoints, and the cost structure below are a good reference. The case study is in the public domain of program management — it is not customer-specific.

## 1. The brief

The brief was submitted in Q3 2025. The hotel group was opening a new flagship property in early 2027 and wanted 100% cashmere throws for the suites and premium rooms. The specification:

- **Volume:** 8,000 pieces total
- **Style:** Throw blanket, 4 size variants
- **Material:** 100% cashmere, 14.5-16.5µm
- **Colorways:** 6 colors, all custom (matched to Pantone TCX with the hotel's interior design team)
- **Construction:** 2-ply woven, 4 cm fringe on two sides
- **Lead time:** 16-20 months from PO to property opening (early 2027)
- **Delivery:** Two batches of 4,000 pieces, six months apart
- **Budget:** Confidential, but the per-piece FOB price was in the $130-180 range depending on the size

The brief included a number of specific requirements: a hand-loomed sample room visit, a 5-piece sample evaluation before the bulk PO, an in-person QC inspection at the mill before the first batch ships, and a custom packaging specification (the throws ship in a reusable cotton bag with the hotel group's logo).

The brief was challenging in three ways: the volume was at the upper end of a single-mill program (8,000 pieces is a substantial program for an Ordos mill), the colorways were all custom (no standing dye-lot program), and the timeline was tight (the first batch needed to ship by Q3 2026 to allow for ocean freight and the property's pre-opening setup).

## 2. The program structure

We structured the program in 4 phases: development, sampling, bulk production batch 1, bulk production batch 2.

**Phase 1: Development (Q3 2025, 6 weeks).** Initial conversation, specification review, sample room visit from the buyer's design team (3 days, 2 designers), and 3 rounds of fabric sample submission. The goal of this phase was to align on the material (2-ply woven in 14.5-16.5µm), the construction (4 cm fringe on two sides), and the colorway process (6 Pantone TCX matches). Total cost: $4,800 in development fees (later credited against the bulk PO).

**Phase 2: Sampling (Q4 2025, 8 weeks).** Five-piece sample evaluation for each of the 6 colorways. The samples were woven on the same looms that would be used for the bulk production. The samples were shipped to the buyer's design team for color matching, hand-feel evaluation, and drape testing. Total cost: $1,800 per colorway (5 pieces × 4 sizes × $90 per piece = $1,800). Total sampling cost: $10,800.

**Phase 3: Bulk production batch 1 (Q1 2026, 18 weeks).** 4,000 pieces across the 4 sizes and 6 colorways. The production was split across 3 weaving lines, each running one size-colorway combination. The QC inspection happened at the mill in Q2 2026 — 3 days, 2 QC specialists from the buyer's team. Total bulk production cost: $640,000 ($160 per piece average).

**Phase 4: Bulk production batch 2 (Q3 2026, 18 weeks).** Another 4,000 pieces with one design change (a longer fringe, 6 cm instead of 4 cm). The design change added 1 week to the production calendar and a $4,000 tooling cost for the new fringe. The QC inspection was remote this time, with the buyer's team using the photos and the lab test results we sent.

**Total program cost: $656,600**, plus the development fees, the sampling fees, the QC inspection costs (the buyer's team travel and time), and the freight ($28,000 for batch 1, $26,000 for batch 2).

## 3. The milestone calendar

The program ran from September 2025 to November 2026, a 14-month active period. The milestones:

- **Q3 2025 (Sep):** Initial brief, design team visit, sample room tour.
- **Q4 2025 (Oct-Dec):** 5-piece sample evaluation, color matching, final specification sign-off.
- **Q1 2026 (Jan-Mar):** PO confirmation, first deposit (30%), production calendar finalized.
- **Q2 2026 (Apr-Jun):** Bulk production batch 1 (4,000 pieces), in-person QC inspection at the mill, second deposit (30%) on shipment.
- **Q3 2026 (Jul-Sep):** Bulk production batch 2 (4,000 pieces), design change incorporated (longer fringe), remote QC inspection.
- **Q4 2026 (Oct-Nov):** Final batch 2 shipment, final deposit (40%), arrival at the property, installation.
- **Early 2027 (planned):** Property opening.

The 14-month active period is on the longer end for a B2B cashmere program. The short version of a similar program (3,000 pieces, 2 sizes, 3 colorways) would be 6-8 months.

## 4. The QC checkpoints

The QC structure for the program was 4 checkpoints, each with a clear pass/fail criterion and a remediation action if the checkpoint failed.

**Checkpoint 1: 5-piece sample evaluation (Q4 2025).** Each colorway was sampled with 5 pieces across the 4 sizes. The acceptance criterion: 4 of 5 pieces per colorway must match the Pantone TCX within ΔE 1.5, and the hand-feel evaluation must be positive (4 of 5 graders, no critical issues). Remediation: re-dye and re-sample, up to 2 cycles. The first cycle passed for 5 of 6 colorways; the 6th colorway (a deep aubergine) needed a 2nd cycle to hit the ΔE 1.5 target.

**Checkpoint 2: In-person QC inspection at the mill (Q2 2026).** The buyer's team spent 3 days at the mill, inspecting 200 pieces across the production batches. The acceptance criterion: defect rate under 1% (i.e. less than 2 of 200 pieces with visible defects). The result: 3 of 200 pieces had visible defects (a small dye streak in 2 pieces, a missed fringe tassel in 1 piece). All 3 were replaced at our cost. The defect rate was 1.5%, which is below our 2% threshold and within the buyer's acceptance range.

**Checkpoint 3: Pre-shipment inspection (Q3 2026 for batch 2).** A random 30-piece sample per colorway per size, inspected for color consistency, fringe uniformity, and size tolerance. The acceptance criterion: all 30 pieces within the spec, no out-of-tolerance issues. The result: 178 of 180 pieces within spec, 2 pieces with a 0.5 cm size deviation (within the 1 cm tolerance). No rejections.

**Checkpoint 4: Incoming inspection at the property (Q4 2026 for batch 2).** The buyer's property team inspected 50 pieces on arrival. The acceptance criterion: no visible defects, no color shift from the original sample, packaging intact. The result: 0 of 50 pieces with defects, packaging intact for all 50.

The 4-checkpoint QC structure caught the small issues (the dye streak, the missed fringe tassel) before the property received the goods. The cost of the QC program was approximately $18,000 across the 14 months (including the buyer's team travel); the cost of the issues that were caught was approximately $4,500 in replacements. The QC program paid for itself.

## 5. The 4 lessons from the program

Looking back at the 14-month program, the 4 lessons that apply to any mid-volume B2B cashmere program are:

**Lesson 1: The dye-lot pre-program saves time and money.** The 6 colorways were all custom Pantone TCX matches. The pre-program (matching the colors, sampling 5 pieces per colorway, and getting the buyer's sign-off) took 8 weeks. The 8 weeks were the highest-leverage 8 weeks in the program — every subsequent production decision was based on the 5-piece sample evaluation, and the 5-piece sample was the buyer's reference for the bulk production. Skipping the sample phase to save 8 weeks would have cost 4-6 weeks of bulk production rework later, plus the trust hit from a quality issue that the sample would have caught.

**Lesson 2: The in-person QC inspection at the mill is the most cost-effective QC investment.** The 3 days at the mill, with 2 QC specialists inspecting 200 pieces, caught 3 defects that would have been missed by a remote inspection. The cost of the inspection (about $8,000 in travel and time) was 2x the cost of the defects (about $4,500 in replacements). But the 2x ratio is misleading — the in-person inspection also caught 4 latent issues (slight fringe length variations, slight color shade shifts in 2 colorways) that the buyer's team decided were within tolerance. The remote QC for batch 2 missed 2 of these latent issues, and the property had to address them in the property's pre-opening setup. The lesson: in-person QC is worth the investment for batch 1; remote QC for batch 2 is fine if the mill's quality has been validated in batch 1.

**Lesson 3: Mid-program design changes are expensive.** The longer-fringe design change added 1 week to the production calendar and $4,000 in tooling. The change was small in scope, but the cost was not small. The reason is that the change had to be applied to all 4,000 pieces in batch 2, plus the buyer's inventory management system had to be updated. The lesson: design changes in the middle of a multi-batch program are 2-3x more expensive than the same change before the program starts. If a design change is likely, push the program start until the change is decided, or split the program into 2 separate single-batch programs.

**Lesson 4: The packaging specification is a real cost.** The reusable cotton bag with the hotel group's logo added $3.20 per piece to the program cost. For an 8,000-piece program, that is $25,600 in packaging alone. The cost was agreed up front and was worth it for the hotel brand experience. But the lesson is that packaging specifications are often an under-discussed cost line in B2B cashmere programs. The packaging is a real cost, and a packaging specification change after the bulk production is even more expensive than a design change.

The raw cashmere fiber is the largest line item, as expected. The dehairing and spinning are 23%, the dyeing 6%, the finishing 4%, and the QC 3%. The total mill-side cost is $840,500; the buyer-side cost (QC + freight) is $62,000. The program margin for us is in the 6-8% range on the mill-side cost.

For comparison, a smaller program (3,000 pieces, 2 sizes, 3 colorways) would have a different cost structure:

- Raw fiber: 91% (similar)
- Processing: 23% (similar)
- Dyeing: 6% (similar)
- Finishing: 4% (similar)
- **QC + certification: 6% (higher, because the per-piece cost is higher)**
- **Mill-side cost as % of program: 70% (vs 92% for 8K pieces)**
- **Freight as % of program: 7% (vs 6% for 8K pieces)**

The smaller program has higher per-piece overhead (QC, freight, development) but the same material and processing structure. The per-piece FOB for a 3,000-piece program is roughly 20-30% higher than for an 8,000-piece program of the same specification.

## 7. The 5 things we would do differently

If we were to run the same program again in 2027, the 5 things we would do differently:

**1. Start the program 8 weeks earlier.** The 14-month active period was tight, especially with the design change. An 8-week buffer would have given us room to address the design change without compressing the QC inspection at the mill.

**2. Lock the packaging specification earlier.** The packaging was the last detail to be finalized, and it was the most expensive to change. Locking it before the sample room visit would have saved time and money.

**3. Use a standing dye-lot program for 4 of the 6 colorways.** The standing dye-lot would have reduced the per-piece cost by $3-4 and shortened the lead time by 2-3 weeks. The 2 custom colorways (the deep aubergine and the muted gold) would have stayed custom.

**4. Add a 1-piece hand-loomed master sample for each colorway.** The 5-piece sample is a bulk-production sample, not a hand-loomed master. A hand-loomed master is more expensive ($200-300 per piece) but it gives the buyer a true reference for the color and the construction. The difference between a 5-piece sample and a 1-piece master is mostly about the buyer's confidence.

**5. Schedule the in-person QC inspection for batch 2 as well.** The remote QC for batch 2 was a cost-saving choice that the buyer made. Looking back, the in-person inspection would have caught the 2 latent issues (the fringe variation and the color shade) that the buyer's property team had to address in the pre-opening setup. The cost of the inspection was $4,000; the cost of the property team addressing the issues was about $12,000. The 3:1 cost ratio says the inspection was the right call.

## 8. The program retrospective

The 8,000-piece throw program for the European hotel group was a successful B2B cashmere program. The hotel opened on time with all 8,000 throws in the suites and the premium rooms. The 6 colorways hit the Pantone TCX targets within ΔE 1.0 (a tighter target than the spec required). The construction met the hand-feel and drape expectations. The packaging was a brand asset for the property, not just a logistics solution.

The program was also a learning experience for us. We learned that the 14-month active period was tight for an 8,000-piece custom program, and that the in-person QC inspection was the highest-leverage quality investment we made. We will use these lessons in the 2027 programs we are already pre-ordering.

If you are planning a similar program — mid-volume (3,000-15,000 pieces), custom colorways, multiple sizes, tight timeline — the lessons from this case study apply directly. The most important ones are:

- The sample phase is the highest-leverage 8 weeks in the program. Do not skip it.
- The in-person QC inspection is the most cost-effective QC investment. Do not skip it.
- The design changes are expensive. Push them to the start of the program, not the middle.
- The packaging is a real cost. Include it in the budget from the start.
- The custom colorways are the biggest single line item. Consider a standing dye-lot program for 50-70% of the colors and custom for the remainder.
