## A recent industry conversation

In 2026, a recurring news cycle has framed cashmere as inherently unsustainable. Articles in trade press and consumer media describe grassland pressure, herd overstocking, and quality decline as if they describe the entire cashmere supply chain. Cashmere industry experts have publicly pushed back on this framing, arguing that the production landscape is more nuanced than a single word, and that blanket characterizations do not help buyers or consumers understand the actual impact of a given shipment.

For a B2B buyer, this debate has a practical consequence. A buyer cannot influence grassland ecology in Inner Mongolia by choosing one word over another on a marketing page, but a buyer *can* ask their supplier for evidence that maps to a specific farm, a specific lot, and a specific program. The remainder of this article is built around that question: what to ask, what evidence to expect, and how to align a contract with the realities on the ground.

> **Buyer principle:** Treat every sustainability or origin claim as a request for evidence. The strongest claims are scoped, dated, and traceable to a transaction.

## Why "unsustainable" is not the right unit of decision

Three distinctions help separate editorial headlines from buying decisions.

1. **Production is not a single unit.** Cashmere is produced across Mongolia, Inner Mongolia (China), Iran, Afghanistan, and other regions, each with different herd sizes, grazing systems, fiber quality, and processing capacity. Inner Mongolia's Ordos and Alashan regions have documented spring combing traditions that differ materially from systems where herders are pressured to increase yields, and from shearing-based systems where fiber length is shorter.
2. **The fiber identity question is downstream of the supply question.** A buyer who cares about fiber origin still needs to verify micron count, length, color, and lot identity. A supplier who cannot document those attributes is unlikely to also be the kind of supplier who can document a credible grazing program. Conversely, a supplier who documents fiber attributes is already partway through a traceability conversation.
3. **Scope matters more than the headline word.** "Sustainable cashmere" without a scope, an audit standard, a validity period, and a defined material flow is a marketing phrase. "Sustainable cashmere certified by [Standard X], scope covering [Farm Y], certificate period [Date], transaction records covering [Lot]" is a request for evidence that a buyer can verify.

These distinctions matter because they move the conversation from politics to procurement. A B2B program should never require a supplier to defend an entire industry; it should ask a supplier to document a specific shipment.

## The questions B2B buyers should put in writing

The following prompts translate the current industry debate into a usable internal checklist. They are designed for written exchange, not for a quick sales call.

| Question | What it tests | Practical buyer follow-up |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Which regions and farms contribute fiber to this lot? | Whether the supplier can scope an origin claim | Ask for the proportion of fiber from each region for the contracted lot |
| What herd size and grazing density is typical for the contributing farms? | Whether the supplier knows the production base, not just the fiber | Compare the numbers against published carrying-capacity references |
| What is the spring combing versus shearing ratio for the contributing fiber? | Whether fiber length is being preserved | Combed fiber is typically longer; sheared fiber often shorter |
| Which independent standard, if any, covers the contributing fiber? | Whether a third party has audited the production base | Request the certificate ID, scope, validity, and audit body |
| What is the transaction record chain from farm to dehairing line? | Whether the supplier can produce material flow records | Ask to see the document for a specific lot, not a generic sample |
| What is the lot-level yield ratio (raw fiber to dehaired fiber)? | Whether the supplier knows what their dehairing line is producing | Compare the ratio against published range; abnormal ratios warrant a question |
| What is the average fiber diameter and length for the lot? | Whether the supplier is operating at a documented quality level | Compare against the agreed specification in the purchase contract |

## What "experts push back" actually tells a buyer

Industry experts who have publicly contested blanket "unsustainable" claims are not arguing that every program is sustainable. They are arguing that the supply base is heterogeneous, that some programs have demonstrable improvements year over year, and that buyers are better served by scope-specific evidence than by generic characterizations.

The practical implication is that the B2B buyer's leverage is in the request, not in the public debate. A request that asks for the certificate holder, the certificate scope, the audit body, the certificate period, and the supporting transaction documents will distinguish a credible supplier from one that has only a slogan. A request that asks "are you sustainable" will get the same answer regardless of supplier, and that answer is the marketing department's view, not the production base's reality.

For programs that target a specific scope (grassland certification, animal welfare, traceability, or social compliance), buyers can require the documentation at the quotation stage and reject quotations that do not produce it. This is far more effective than negotiating the wording of a marketing claim after the goods have shipped.

## What to require in the contract

The contract is where the request becomes binding. Three clauses consistently outperform rhetoric.

1. **Lot-level origin declaration.** A clause that requires the supplier to declare, for each shipment, the fiber's region of origin, the contributing farms by name and proportion, and the dehairing or spinning batch reference. This converts "Alashan goat" into a document with a name and a quantity.
2. **Independent standard coverage clause.** If the program targets a specific standard (such as the Sustainable Fibre Alliance's Animal Fibre Standard, the Good Cashmere Standard, or another independent scheme), the clause should reference the standard by name, cite the certificate ID at the time of quotation, and require notification if the certificate lapses or is suspended during the contract term.
3. **Acceptance rule for missing or inconsistent documentation.** A clause that says what happens if documentation is missing or inconsistent — hold for review, re-test, downgrade, rework, or reject. Without this clause, the documentation request is optional in practice.

## A practical buyer checklist for the current debate

1. **Ask for scope, not adjectives.** "Which standard, which scope, which period?" is more useful than "are you sustainable."
2. **Ask for the lot-level documents, not the brochure.** Origin declaration, dehairing batch reference, transaction records, and any certificate ID for the contracted lot are.
4. **Ask for the fiber data alongside the sustainability claim.** Diameter, length, and yield ratio are evidence of a supplier who knows what their production line is doing.
4. **Anchor the contract to documentation, not to wording.** Lot-level origin, standard coverage, and a documented acceptance rule convert a marketing conversation into a procurement one.
5. **Treat regional context as information, not as judgment.** Inner Mongolia, Mongolia, Iran, and Afghanistan each have distinct production bases. The right question is whether the supplier's program is documented for the fiber in your shipment, not which region is "best."

## Final takeaway

A blanket "unsustainable cashmere" claim is not a decision-making tool for a B2B program. A scope-specific request for evidence is. Cashmere industry experts have publicly pushed back on the blanket framing because it does not help a buyer or a consumer understand a real shipment, and because the production base is heterogeneous. The buyer who asks for the certificate scope, the audit body, the lot-level origin declaration, and the fiber data alongside any sustainability claim will arrive at a procurement decision that is more accurate than the editorial conversation can support.
