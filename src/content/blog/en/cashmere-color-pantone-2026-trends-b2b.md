## 2. Why these colors work technically on cashmere

A cashmere yarn that is fine (16-19 micron) and well-dehaired takes saturated dye more cleanly than a coarser yarn, because there is less surface variation and fiber-to-fiber light scatter. This is why the FW26 colors that are showing up most are colors that test well on fine-gauge knitwear — saturated mid-tone colors (teal, navy, red) and warm neutrals (off-white, pale yellow, coral).

There are three color categories that perform poorly on cashmere and rarely appear in our color-matching briefs:

- **Very light pastels** (under 5% dye saturation): show fiber variation and yarn irregularities too clearly. They require a higher-grade fiber and are typically only spec'd for premium yarn.
- **Pure black** (Pantone 19-3911): requires multiple dye baths, weakens the fiber, and is the color most associated with cashmere pilling. Most premium cashmere programs avoid pure black in favor of dark navy, charcoal, or very dark brown.
- **Bright fluorescent colors**: outside the cashmere industry's normal performance envelope. Some vendors will accept the brief; the fiber tends to feel harsher after the dye process.

If your collection requires one of these three categories, expect to negotiate MOQ upward (because the dye yield is lower) and confirm the fiber hand-feel tolerance in advance.

## 3. The undyed cashmere comeback

The most under-reported color trend in cashmere for 2026 is the quiet return of undyed (greige) cashmere — fiber that is left in its natural color after dehairing and is not bleached or dyed. There are three natural cashmere colors:

- **White cashmere** (from white Alashan and Ordos goats): the most dyeable, traditionally reserved for pastels and saturated shades
- **Brown cashmere / 'qing'** (from brown goats): warm mid-tone, naturally beige-brown
- **Purple cashmere / 'zi'** (from grey-purple goats): the rarest and most expensive natural color

Undyed cashmere has a 25-35% lower price per kilo than bleached-and-dyed cashmere (no dye cost, no bleach, no color-matching trial cost), and the fiber retains more of its natural softness because it has not been through the additional chemical baths. The trade-off is that you are limited to the natural palette plus overdyes in compatible tones.

Why is undyed making a comeback in 2026? Two reasons:

1. **Sustainability positioning**: bleached undyed reduces chemical use by 70-80% compared to bleached-and-dyed, which fits the EU Green Claims Directive (in force September 2026) and the buyer demand for traceable, low-impact fiber
2. **Aesthetic preference for the natural look**: Pantone Cloud Dancer (off-white) is the most-requested Pantone reference in our FW26 briefs, and an undyed white cashmere can hit Cloud Dancer without any dye bath at all

We offer undyed white, brown, and purple cashmere as stock fiber (MOQ 10kg per color, no dye MOQ required). For bleached-and-dyed or Pantone-matched custom dye, see section 4.

## 4. What to confirm with your factory on Pantone-matched custom dye

If your brief requires a Pantone-matched color that is not in your supplier's stock yarn color card, the process is custom dye. There are five practical points to confirm before committing:

**MOQ.** Custom dye on cashmere typically requires 30-50kg per color minimum at the yarn dyeing stage, which translates to roughly 100-200 finished garments depending on yarn count and garment weight. If your collection volume is below that threshold, you have three options: (1) use a supplier's stock yarn color that is visually close to the Pantone reference, (2) combine stock colors (e.g., 60% stock-color-A + 40% stock-color-B) to approximate the Pantone reference, or (3) commission a smaller-lot dye run at a 20-30% price premium per kilo.

**Color-matching tolerance.** Standard Pantone-matching tolerance is Delta E ≤ 1.0 against the master color standard, measured under D65 lighting. Tighter tolerance (Delta E ≤ 0.5) is achievable but adds 10-15% to the dye cost and lengthens the sampling timeline. Confirm the tolerance in writing before sampling; verbal 'we can match Pantone' without specifying Delta E is the most common cause of color-rejection at pre-shipment inspection.

**Dye method.** The four most common cashmere dye methods are acid dyes (the standard for protein fibers), reactive dyes (slightly better colorfastness, slightly higher cost), low-impact reactive dyes (the EU-preferred option for the Green Claims Directive), and natural/plant dyes (premium positioning, lower colorfastness, 30-50% cost premium). For most cashmere B2B buyers, low-impact reactive is the default recommendation in 2026 because it aligns with EU buyer requirements and adds only 5-8% to the dye cost over standard acid dyes.

**Same Pantone reference, different yarn counts = different visual color.** A 2/26 Nm yarn in Pantone Navy Blue is a noticeably different visual color than a 2/48 Nm yarn in the same Pantone Navy Blue, because the finer yarn has more fiber-to-fiber light scatter. If your collection includes multiple yarn counts in the same color, plan for separate color-matching trials per yarn count.

**Lead time.** A standard Pantone-matching sample cycle (lab dip → buyer approval → bulk dye) takes 15-25 working days. Plan it into your development timeline from tech-pack approval, not from bulk order confirmation, because the dye trial often runs in parallel with the yarn order.
