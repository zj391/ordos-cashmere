The cashmere maintenance questions that come up most often in 2026 are not the end-consumer care questions — those are well-covered by every cashmere brand's care card. The questions that come up are the wholesale buyer's: how do I store the cashmere when it arrives from the Ordos mill, how do I ship it to my retail customer, and how do I handle the after-sale support questions that flow back to me from the end consumer.

This page is the wholesale version of the cashmere care guide. It covers pre-retail storage at the brand warehouse, long-haul shipping to retail customers, the after-sale support questions, and the inventory management issues that distinguish a cashmere program from a wool or cotton program. None of it is a replacement for the end-consumer care card — for that, see the 2026 Winter Cashmere Care Guide. This is the upstream version that the wholesale buyer needs to know.

## 1. The pre-retail storage window

The typical wholesale cashmere program has a 30-90 day pre-retail storage window: the mill ships the bulk production, the brand warehouse receives it, the brand inspects and packs, and the orders are fulfilled to retail customers or store locations. This is the period where cashmere is most vulnerable to the four main storage risks: humidity, moth, compression, and light.

**Humidity.** Cashmere fiber is hygroscopic — it absorbs and releases moisture from the surrounding air. The ideal storage humidity for cashmere is 50-55% relative humidity. Below 40%, the fiber dries out and becomes brittle. Above 60%, the fiber absorbs too much moisture and is at risk of mildew. The damage is not visible immediately; a cashmere sweater stored at 75% humidity for 60 days will show the mildew in 6-12 months when the customer wears it.

The fix: climate-controlled warehouse. If your warehouse is in a humid climate (Southeast US, southern Japan, Mediterranean, southern China), the air conditioning should be on, and you should have a dehumidifier in the cashmere storage area. If your warehouse is in a dry climate (US Mountain West, northern China, central Europe), the heating should be on, and you should have a humidifier in the cashmere storage area. The humidity monitor is the cheapest insurance you can buy.

**Moth.** Cashmere is a protein fiber, and clothes moths (Tineola bisselliella) and carpet beetles (Dermestidae) eat protein fibers. The moth is the bigger risk because the larva can eat through a cashmere garment in 2-3 weeks if left undisturbed. The fix: cedar blocks or lavender sachets in the storage area (NOT mothballs — the smell is permanent and toxic). The more robust fix: sealed plastic bins with a few drops of cedar oil. The most robust fix: a professional pest-control contract that includes quarterly inspections.

**Compression.** Cashmere garments stored under heavy weight (such as stacked under other inventory) can develop permanent compression marks. The marks are usually recoverable with a steam press, but the recovery is not 100%. The fix: store cashmere on hangers (for sweaters and cardigans) or flat-folded with acid-free tissue between layers (for scarves and blankets). For bulk storage in cardboard boxes, line the box with acid-free tissue and limit the stack to 5 garments.

**Light.** Direct sunlight fades cashmere color over 4-6 weeks. The fix: store cashmere in opaque boxes or in a dark corner of the warehouse. Most brands use opaque cotton garment bags for in-warehouse storage; the bags protect from dust, light, and minor compression.

The 30-90 day pre-retail window is not a long time. With reasonable storage conditions, cashmere is stable. The risk increases if the window stretches to 6+ months — at that point, even good storage conditions start to show some fiber degradation.

## 2. Receiving cashmere from the mill

When the mill ships the cashmere (typically by ocean freight in a corrugated cardboard carton, vacuum-packed or polybagged), the receiving process at the brand warehouse should follow a 5-step protocol. We provide this protocol to all wholesale clients.

**Step 1: Inspect the carton condition before opening.** Look for water damage, puncture, or unusual weight. If the carton has visible water damage, photograph it before opening (for the shipping insurance claim) and open in a clean area to assess the garment damage.

**Step 2: Open in a clean area and count units.** Confirm the unit count against the packing list. If there is a discrepancy, document immediately.

**Step 3: Visual inspection of each unit.** Look for stains, holes, color inconsistencies, and sizing inconsistencies. If a unit has any of these, set it aside for the QC team to evaluate. Most reputable mills have an allowance of 1-3% for visible defects; the defect rate above 5% is a conversation with the mill.

**Step 4: Random QC inspection.** Pull 5-10% of the units and inspect them against the original spec. For a 5,000-piece PO, inspect 250-500 pieces. The random sample should match the original sample (lab dip for color, first 5 pieces for silhouette and gauge). If the random sample does not match, the rest of the lot is suspect.

**Step 5: Storage decision.** If the lot passes inspection, store per the conditions in section 1. If it does not, quarantine the lot and open a QC conversation with the mill within 48 hours. The mill's response window is typically 5-7 business days for an investigation; the resolution window is 14-30 days for a re-do or a credit.

The 5-step protocol takes about 4-6 hours per 1,000 pieces for a warehouse team of 2-3 people. The investment in the protocol pays off in lower defect rates at retail, lower return rates from end consumers, and a faster QC loop with the mill.

## 3. Long-haul shipping to retail

Once the cashmere is in the brand warehouse and the orders are being fulfilled, the shipping question becomes relevant. The two main shipping modes are ocean freight (3-6 weeks) and air freight (3-7 days). Both have specific cashmere considerations.

**Ocean freight.** The standard mode for cashmere wholesale shipments from Ordos to retail warehouses in North America, Europe, or Japan. The key consideration is moisture control. A standard 40-foot shipping container experiences temperature swings of 10-30°C during a trans-Pacific transit, with humidity swings of 40-90%. The humidity swing is the bigger risk — at 90% humidity for 30 days, the cashmere can develop the start of mildew that does not show until 6-12 months later.

The fix: desiccant bags in each shipping carton. The standard practice is 4-6 desiccant bags (50g silica gel) per 12-piece carton. The bags absorb the humidity spikes during transit. Cost is roughly $0.10-0.30 per garment; the cost is trivial compared to the risk.

**Air freight.** Used for reorders, late deliveries, and small-lot programs. The transit time is 3-7 days, which means the humidity exposure is much shorter. The main risk with air freight is compression — air freight tends to use heavier cartons with more aggressive stacking, and the compression marks on a cashmere garment are visible at retail.

The fix: ask the freight forwarder for "soft stack" or "no double-stack" handling. The cost premium is $50-150 per shipment; the result is fewer compression-mark issues at retail.

For both modes, the brand warehouse should do an incoming inspection on the cashmere as soon as the cartons arrive. The 5-step protocol from section 2 applies, with the additional check that the desiccant bags are still dry (they should be — if they are saturated, the transit conditions were worse than expected).

## 4. The after-sale support questions

The 8 most common after-sale support questions that flow back to wholesale buyers from end consumers in 2026, in roughly the order of frequency:

**Q1: "How do I wash this?"** Answer: cold water (20°C or below), pH-neutral wool wash, hand wash or delicate cycle in a mesh laundry bag, lay flat to dry. The end-consumer care card should cover this. If the care card is missing, redirect to the brand's standard care guidance, not to the mill.

**Q2: "Is this real cashmere?"** Answer: yes, with the OEKO-TEX or other certification number on the care label. If the buyer needs to verify, the mill can provide a 100g fiber sample from the same lot for comparison (this is unusual but happens for high-end resellers).

**Q3: "Why is it pilling?"** Answer: pilling in the first 2-4 wears is normal and is a function of loose surface fibers. After 5-6 wears, pilling should reduce with a cashmere comb. Persistent pilling beyond 5-6 wears may indicate a yarn quality issue, in which case the brand should investigate with the mill.

**Q4: "The color is fading."** Answer: most natural-fiber dyes have a 1-2% color shift in the first 5 washes. If the fading is dramatic, it may be an issue with the dye lot or the wash water (chlorine, hard water, hot water). The brand should ask the consumer to provide a photo of the original color and the faded color to assess.

**Q5: "There's a hole in it."** Answer: a hole is either a moth damage (consumer-side issue), a manufacturing defect (mill-side issue), or a wear-through from friction (consumer-side issue). The brand's QC team should look at the photo and the lot history to determine the source.

**Q6: "Can I dry clean it?"** Answer: yes, but with restrictions. Standard dry cleaning is fine for cashmere. Trichloroethylene-based dry cleaning is fine. Perchloroethylene-based dry cleaning is the industry standard and is fine. Wet cleaning is also fine if the cleaner has cashmere experience. The most common dry-cleaning damage to cashmere is over-pressing, which flattens the fiber and reduces softness.

**Q7: "How do I store it in summer?"** Answer: clean before storage, fold (don't hang), store in breathable cotton (not plastic), add cedar or lavender, store in a dark closet. This is the "summer storage" question that comes up every spring.

**Q8: "What if it shrinks?"** Answer: cashmere shrinks when washed in hot water or dried in a dryer. If the consumer did this, the shrinkage is permanent and there is no recovery. If the consumer did not, the shrinkage is likely a manufacturing issue (yarn was over-twisted or finished at too high a temperature) and the brand should investigate with the mill.

For Q3, Q4, Q5, and Q8, the wholesale buyer's role is to be the bridge between the consumer and the mill. The mill can provide a 100g fiber sample from the same lot for the consumer to compare, or a defect analysis from the same lot to determine the likely cause. The wholesale buyer's role is to make the handoff smoothly and to track the cases so that patterns (e.g. 5% of units in a particular lot have an issue) get caught early.

## 5. Inventory management for cashmere

Cashmere inventory management is different from wool or cotton inventory management for three reasons:

**Cashmere is a high-value, low-volume item.** A typical B2B cashmere program is 5,000-50,000 pieces per year. A typical B2B cotton program is 100,000-500,000 pieces per year. The cashmere program needs more careful unit-level tracking because the per-piece cost is higher and the impact of an inventory error is larger.

**Cashmere has a longer design-to-shelf cycle.** A cashmere program that ships in Q3 2027 needs design and PO in Q4 2026, with sample room iterations in Q2-Q3 2026. A cotton program that ships in Q3 2027 needs design in Q1 2027, with sample room iterations in Q1 2027. The cashmere program needs longer forecasting windows.

**Cashmere has higher replacement costs.** If a cashmere garment is damaged in the warehouse (moth, mildew, compression, color shift), the brand has to either repair the garment (which is expensive — $10-20 per piece for a quality repair) or replace it from the mill (which has a 30-45 day lead time). If a cotton garment is damaged, the brand can replace it from a local supplier in 2-3 weeks at a fraction of the cost.

The practical implication: cashmere programs need better inventory visibility than cotton or wool programs. The wholesale buyer should have:

- A unit-level inventory system (not just SKU-level) so that damaged units can be tracked back to the lot, the mill, the dye lot, the production period.
- A FIFO (first in, first out) rotation system for the cashmere inventory so that older stock goes out first.
- A 60-day inventory health check for any cashmere in the warehouse for more than 90 days. The check is visual inspection + 5-piece lab test for color shift.
- A pre-allocated replacement budget for damaged units. The typical budget is 3-5% of the cashmere inventory value.

For a brand that is not yet doing these four things, the cost of cashmere inventory management is higher than the cost of doing it. The break-even on a unit-level inventory system is usually 1-2 seasons of cashmere inventory.

## 6. The seasonal calendar for cashmere

Cashmere has a predictable seasonal calendar for wholesale buyers. Understanding the calendar helps with inventory planning and consumer messaging.

**Q1 (Jan-Mar):** FW delivery season. The fall/winter collection ships to retail. The wholesale buyer's job is to manage the inventory ramp-down of FW stock and the inventory ramp-up of SS stock.

**Q2 (Apr-Jun):** SS delivery season. The spring/summer collection ships to retail. The wholesale buyer's job is to manage the SS inventory and the end-of-FW markdown season. The Q2 is also the period for the consumer to ask the summer storage questions — see Q7 in section 4.

**Q3 (Jul-Sep):** FW development season. The fall/winter program is being designed, sampled, and approved. The wholesale buyer's job is to coordinate with the mill on the sample room and the pre-order. This is also the consumer to ask the "how do I store my cashmere" question, which the wholesale buyer should have a standard answer for.

**Q4 (Oct-Dec):** FW pre-order season. The fall/winter program is being placed as a PO. The wholesale buyer's job is to confirm the pre-order with the mill, finalize the production calendar, and prepare for the Q1 FW delivery. The Q4 is also the holiday gifting season — cashmere is a high-end gift, and the brand's holiday messaging should highlight the cashmere program.

The seasonal calendar is predictable enough that a wholesale buyer can plan the inventory and the consumer messaging 12 months in advance. The risk is in the Q3-Q4 transition, when the new FW program is being designed and the old FW inventory is being cleared. This is the period when a brand's cashmere program can get out of sync with the consumer demand.

A practical checklist for the wholesale buyer, by month:

- **Jan:** FW sell-through review; reorder analysis; consumer feedback summary.
- **Feb:** FW markdown planning; SS pre-development.
- **Mar:** SS sample room; FW replacement orders; pre-FW year-end plan.
- **Apr:** SS sample room; FW closeout; SS pre-orders.
- **May:** SS PO confirm; consumer summer storage content; moth-prevention prep.
- **Jun:** SS pre-orders close; SS sample room; mid-year QC review.
- **Jul:** SS sell-in; FW sample room; SS replacement orders.
- **Aug:** FW sample room; SS sell-through; pre-FW consumer content.
- **Sep:** FW pre-orders; SS markdown; FW sample room.
- **Oct:** FW pre-orders close; holiday gift prep; FW production calendar.
- **Nov:** FW production start; SS closeout; holiday sell-through.
- **Dec:** FW production; year-end review; SS development plan.

The checklist is not exhaustive — every brand will have variations. But it captures the rhythm of a cashmere B2B program, and it is the kind of calendar that separates a stable cashmere program from one that has inventory surprises every season.
