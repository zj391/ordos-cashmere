# Inside an Ordos Cashmere Factory: What B2B Buyers Actually See on a Factory Tour

A factory tour is the single highest-ROI hour you can spend before placing a USD 50K+ cashmere order. Email exchanges, sample shipments, and Zoom meetings all help, but walking a facility tells you things no questionnaire reveals: how workers actually behave when the owner isn't looking, whether the dehairing line is calibrated or running 10-year-old settings, whether the warehouse is organized or stuffed floor-to-ceiling. We've hosted 200+ buyers at our Ordos facility over the last decade and walked through 30+ competitor factories as part of industry events. This guide explains what to look for, what questions to ask, and what red flags should send you home.

If you're evaluating a Chinese cashmere supplier for the first time, plan to spend 2-3 days: one full day at the factory, plus 1-2 days visiting fiber suppliers, dye houses, or finishing facilities. The full tour described below fits in one full day with a serious buyer. Most legitimate factories accommodate visits within 2-3 weeks notice — longer lead times or refusal to host are themselves red flags.

## Before You Arrive: What to Request from the Factory

Send this list to the factory 1-2 weeks before your visit. Their willingness to share is itself a signal:

- **Floor plan** — so you know which areas you'll see and which you won't
- **Worker count** (peak season vs. off-season)
- **Machinery list** — brand, model, year of major equipment (dehairing, spinning, knitting)
- **Recent client references** (3-5 brands with permission to share)
- **Audit history** — any SGS, Bureau Veritas, BSCI, or SA8000 audits from the last 3 years
- **Capacity utilization** — percentage of full capacity they're running today (anything below 40% signals weak demand or recent client losses)
- **Sample swatches** of their current fiber, yarn, and finished goods

**Red flag before you arrive**: Factory refuses to share machinery list, capacity utilization, or recent client references. Walk away before booking the flight.

## Hour 1: Office Tour and Owner/Manager Meeting

The first hour sets the tone. You'll typically start in the front office, meet the owner or general manager, and see sample displays of yarn and finished products.

**What to look for**:

- **Owner involvement**: Is the owner actively in the office during your visit, or do they delegate to a sales manager? Owner-involved factories (especially family-owned) typically have better quality control because decisions get made on the floor, not in committee. **Red flag**: Owner is "traveling" but you see their luxury car parked outside. Suggests they're hiding something.
- **Sample organization**: Yarn cones, fabric swatches, and finished garment samples should be organized by yarn count, micron, and season. Chaotic samples suggest chaotic operations.
- **Office staff presence**: 3-5 office staff for a mid-sized factory is normal. An empty office (just you and one sales person) suggests the factory doesn't have the sales or finance depth to manage ongoing B2B relationships.
- **Sample readiness**: Can they pull a stock yarn cone or sample garment from the shelf in 60 seconds? Or do they have to go to the warehouse? Both are fine, but the former signals efficient sample handling.

**Questions to ask**:
- How long have you been operating this facility? (5+ years is normal; <3 years is risky)
- What's your average client retention rate? (70%+ is healthy)
- What's your largest single-client concentration? (Under 30% is healthier)
- Which export markets do you serve? (Diversified = lower risk)
- Can I see your export license and business registration? (Legitimate factories have these)

## Hour 2: Fiber Warehouse and Dehairing Line

The fiber warehouse reveals whether the factory is honestly sourcing what they sell. Then you walk the dehairing line — the critical step that turns raw cashmere (with guard hair contamination) into usable fiber.

**Fiber warehouse**:

- **Fiber bag labels**: Each bag should have a tag with combing station name, harvest date, micron range, and grade. Generic unlabeled bags = unverifiable provenance.
- **Inventory turnover**: Are new fiber arrivals mixed with old stock (suggesting slow turnover and aging fiber)? Or is there a clear rotation?
- **Storage conditions**: Fiber should be in a dry, ventilated space. Humid or musty storage degrades fiber over time.

**Dehairing line**:

- **Equipment**: Look for make and model nameplates. Italian-made (Carding Machine Services, Cormatex) or quality Chinese (Shanghai Johnny, Shenyang Textile Machinery) dehairing machines are standard. Very old machines or homemade equipment signal cost-cutting.
- **Worker skill**: Workers should be feeding fiber at a controlled rate, not rushing. Quick tension changes or fiber jams mean the line is being pushed past its capacity.
- **Output quality**: The dehaired fiber coming off the line should look clean, uniformly fine, and free of guard hair. Visible guard hair means poor calibration.
- **Dust and noise**: Dehairing generates dust and noise. Some dust is normal; visible clouds of dust in the air mean inadequate ventilation and worker safety risk.

**Red flag**: The factory has only one dehairing line. Most mid-sized factories need 2-3 dehairing lines to handle volume. A single line limits capacity and creates a single point of failure.

## Hour 3: Spinning and Yarn Production

Not every cashmere factory spins its own yarn — many buy yarn from regional spinners. Either approach is legitimate, but the answers to your questions differ.

**If the factory spins yarn in-house**:

- **Spinning frames**: Look for Italian (Gaudino, Savio), German (Zinser), or quality Chinese (Tongxiang) frames. Older mechanical frames (< 1990s) signal aging capacity.
- **Yarn cone storage**: Yarn cones should be stored by count, lot, and color in organized bins. Mixed storage means quality control problems.
- **Lot traceability**: Each cone should trace to a fiber lot, spinning run, and QC record. If they can't trace a cone back to its origin fiber in 60 seconds, their lot control is weak.

**If the factory buys yarn externally**:

- **Yarn supplier relationships**: Ask which spinning mills they source from. Three quality mills (e.g., ERDOS Group's spinning division, Hubei Chunfeng, Zhejiang Xinao) is normal. If they can't name a specific mill, they may be buying from low-quality sources.

**Red flag**: Factory claims to spin in-house but can't show spinning equipment, or claims "directly from fiber to yarn" with no intermediate steps. This is usually a sign they're reselling yarn from another spinner and adding markup.

## Hour 4: Knitting Floor and Cut-and-Sew

This is where most production capacity and labor risk lives. Plan to spend at least 45 minutes here.

**Knitting floor**:

- **Machine count**: 10-30 machines for a mid-sized factory is normal. Fewer than 8 means limited capacity.
- **Machine brands**: STOLL (Germany), Shima Seiki (Japan), Protti (Germany) are premium. Chinese brands (Longxing, Wuxi) are acceptable for stockinette.
- **Machine age and maintenance**: Look for oil stains, rust, broken needles. Well-maintained machines look clean and busy.
- **Operator skill**: Workers should sit at machines with finished garments beside them, not running back and forth to a supervisor.

**Cut-and-sew workshop**:

- **Worker spacing**: Workers should have minimum 1.2 m² each (Chinese labor law). Crowded workshops = quality risk + labor compliance risk.
- **Lighting and ventilation**: Adequate lighting (essential for color matching and stitch inspection). Visible ventilation fans or HVAC.
- **Fire safety**: Fire extinguishers at intervals, emergency exits clearly marked, no blocked exits. Missing = compliance failure.
- **Worker age and demeanor**: No visible child workers. Workers should look alert, not exhausted.

**Red flag**: All sewing machines are old mechanical models (vs. modern servo-controlled). The factory is producing at the lowest end of the quality spectrum — fine for fast fashion, not for premium cashmere.

## Hour 5: Quality Control Lab and Warehouse

The QC lab determines whether the factory can verify its own claims about fiber grade, yarn quality, and garment durability.

**What should be in a serious QC lab**:

- **Fiber diameter analyzer** (OFDA or similar) — measures fiber diameter distribution. Without this, the factory can't validate its 14.5μm claims.
- **Pilling tester** (Martindale or ICI) — simulates wear cycles to test pilling. Critical for cashmere garments.
- **Color fastness testing** — wash, light, and rub fastness.
- **Hand-feel reference samples** — yarn and fabric samples organized by quality grade.
- **Calibration records** — equipment should have current calibration stickers (typically annual calibration).

**Warehouse**:

- **Organization**: Yarn, fabric, and finished goods stored in distinct zones with clear labeling. Mix of unlabeled cartons, half-empty pallets, and crowded floors = poor inventory management.
- **Finished goods age**: Look at the date codes on cartons. If you see cartons from 2023 in a busy factory, either inventory is moving slowly (slow sales) or it's old stock being relabeled (red flag for old product resold as new).
- **Pest control**: Cashmere attracts moths and silverfish. Look for pest control evidence (traps, sealed storage, no visible damage to fiber bags).

**Red flag**: No QC lab visible, or lab equipment looks 15+ years old and uncalibrated. Factory can't validate its own quality claims.

## Hour 6: Lunch, Owner Q&A, and Closing Discussion

Most factory tours wrap up with a working lunch in the factory canteen or a nearby restaurant. This is your chance for a more candid conversation with the owner or GM.

**What to evaluate**:

- **Canteen quality**: Worker food quality signals how the owner treats their workforce. A clean canteen with hot food = decent labor practices. No canteen (workers eat off-site) = possible cost-cutting.
- **Owner transparency**: Does the owner answer questions directly, or deflect to "let me check with my team"? Good owners answer even uncomfortable questions (e.g., "What's your defect rate?" — should be under 3% for finished garments).
- **Worker interviews**: If you have time and language support, interview 2-3 workers (anonymously) about hours, pay, and treatment. Long hours or complaints about wage delays = red flags.

**Questions to wrap up with**:

- Can I see your defect/return rate over the last 12 months? (Healthy factories share this; defensive factories won't)
- What's your on-time delivery rate for the last 12 months? (Should be 90%+ for finished goods)
- Who are your top 3 competitors and why should I work with you instead?
- What's your biggest customer pain point and how are you addressing it?

The answers to these last questions reveal more than 10 hours of facility walkthrough. A factory with 95% on-time delivery, 2% defect rate, and a clear competitive story is worth following up with a sample order. A factory that hedges every question is not.

## Eight Red Flags That Should Make You Walk Away

After 200+ factory visits, these are the eight red flags that, taken together or alone, suggest the factory is not a serious supplier:

1. **Owner not present and refuses to commit a meeting time during your visit**. Either they're avoiding you or they don't care enough to make the time.

2. **Refusal to walk certain areas** ("warehouse is too messy right now" or "we're not allowed"). Legitimate factories have nothing to hide in areas that affect quality.

3. **No fiber traceability documentation**. Bag labels are absent or generic. You can't verify where their cashmere actually came from.

4. **Machinery is 15+ years old across the board**. They're running on depreciated equipment and may not have the cash flow to invest in upgrades.

5. **Single dehairing line**. Capacity is limited, and you have no fallback if it breaks down during your production run.

6. **No QC lab or visibly uncalibrated equipment**. They can't validate the quality they claim.

7. **Worker conditions fail basic inspection** (crowded, blocked exits, no fire safety). Compliance audit failure is a near-certainty for your downstream brand.

8. **Owner or sales staff deflects every direct question**. Vague answers about defect rates, delivery performance, or client retention signal they're hiding something.

One red flag is forgivable. Two warrants a follow-up investigation. Three or more — walk away.

## Virtual Factory Tour: When You Can't Travel

If a China trip isn't feasible (cost, visa, time), request a virtual tour. Most legitimate factories accommodate this within 2-3 days notice. What to ask for:

- **Live video walk-through** via WhatsApp or Zoom — 30-45 minutes, with you asking questions in real time. The factory should walk you through the same 6-hour tour described above, condensed.
- **Worker count visible on camera**: If the "factory" shows 5 workers total, it's not a real factory.
- **Equipment nameplates visible**: Camera should show machinery brand and model nameplates at some point. If they don't, ask.
- **Owner present on camera**: A factory that sends only sales staff for the virtual tour (no owner or floor manager) is hiding something.

A virtual tour is 60-70% as informative as an in-person tour. It's acceptable for an initial filter; less so for a USD 50K+ first order.

## After the Tour: The 14-Day Decision Window

After visiting, give yourself 14 days to make a decision. During that window:

- **Day 1-3**: Send a thank-you note. Reference 1-2 specific observations from the tour to confirm you actually paid attention.
- **Day 4-7**: Request 3-5 yarn samples and 2-3 garment samples for lab testing. Independent lab test (SGS, Bureau Veritas, or local university textile lab) costs USD 200-500 and validates the factory's claims.
- **Day 8-10**: Reference call — ask the factory for 2-3 client references (or call known brands you've seen in their sample cards). Email the references directly.
- **Day 11-14**: Decision. Either proceed to a USD 5K-10K trial order with a 70/30 payment split (70% after delivery confirmation), or move to the next factory on your list.

If you've done this diligence, the trial order is low-risk. If the factory passes the trial, you have a supplier for the next 5+ years. If not, you've lost USD 5K-10K and learned what doesn't work — still cheaper than placing a USD 50K order with the wrong factory.
