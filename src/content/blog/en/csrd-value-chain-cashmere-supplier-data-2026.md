# CSRD for Cashmere Buyers: How EU Reporting Rules Reach Your Supplier's Data

A European brand buying cashmere knitwear may now ask you a question that has nothing to do with your product: *what is the carbon figure for this order, and can you evidence it?*

That question arrives through the **Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD)** and the **European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS)** that sit underneath it. Neither instrument names a cashmere factory. Both of them nonetheless reach one.

This note sets out where the CSRD actually stands in late October 2026 — after a year in which it was significantly simplified — and then follows the reporting obligation backwards into the supply chain, because that is where a supplier meets it.

## Where the CSRD actually stands

The CSRD has had an unusually turbulent two years, and most summaries of it are now out of date in one direction or the other. Here is the position as of this writing.

**The original framework.** The CSRD entered into force in January 2023. Companies above a size threshold and companies listed on a regulated market report on their social and environmental risks and impacts. The first wave reported on financial year 2024, with reports published during 2025.

**The simplification, February 2025.** The Commission's **Omnibus** legislative package proposed in February 2025 to cut the scope back sharply — most consequentially to apply the CSRD **only to companies with more than 1,000 employees**, concentrating reporting on the companies with the largest impacts, and to reduce the burden on smaller companies in those companies' own value chains.

**Stop-the-clock, April 2025.** In April, Parliament and Council reached political agreement on a directive postponing the application date for the companies that would otherwise have reported for the first time on financial years 2025 and 2026 — wave two and wave three.

**ESRS quick-fix, July 2025.** A delegated act adopted on 11 July 2025 revised the first set of ESRS. It gave wave one companies additional flexibility, and confirmed they do **not** have to report additional information for financial years 2025 and 2026 beyond what they already reported for 2024.

**Omnibus I, December 2025.** Political agreement between Parliament and Council on the Omnibus I simplification package on 9 December 2025.

**ESRS simplification, July 2026.** On 3 July 2026, two delegated acts were adopted. One amends Delegated Regulation (EU) 2023/2772 to simplify certain sustainability reporting standards. The other supplements Directive 2013/34/EU by establishing sustainability reporting standards for **voluntary** use by undertakings protected by the value chain cap.

**The SME route.** On 30 July 2025, EFRAG issued a recommendation for a **Voluntary Sustainability Reporting Standard for SMEs (VSME)** — a simplified voluntary standard intended for smaller undertakings in the chain.

If you follow the thread, the direction of travel is unmistakable: the reporting burden has been narrowed to the very largest companies, the standards have been made less demanding for the first wave, and a lighter voluntary route exists for the SMEs below.

## Why a cashmere supplier still gets asked

Here is where the "but it only applies to companies with 1,000 employees" argument runs into the actual mechanics.

A large brand that *is* in scope has a reporting obligation that its own ESRS disclosures depend on. To produce a value-chain disclosure — and this is the part that touches you — the reporting company has to understand the social and environmental characteristics of the value chain behind its own operations. The ESRS framework addresses this directly, and the practical methods for building that picture are the ones that generate supplier questionnaires:

- **Value chain mapping.** Which tiers of the supply chain are in scope for the disclosure. A brand with a tier-1 and tier-2 requirement reaches you at tier 1.
- **Data collection from value chain entities.** Directly, or by proxy through an industry association, a recognised methodology, or a value-chain operator that has itself reported. A supplier that is genuinely transparent about its own operations is far easier to work with than one that is not.
- **Estimation where data is unavailable.** Where the reporting company cannot obtain data, it must estimate. Estimation is allowed, but it is disclosed as an estimate, and it is weaker evidence than a measured figure.

The direction of travel in the simplified CSRD is to *reduce* how far the obligation reaches into smaller suppliers. It has not removed the need for a large brand to be able to describe its supply chain at all.

**The practical result:** a European brand's CSRD work turns into a set of specific, answerable questions to its suppliers. Which is exactly where you meet it.

## What a supplier is actually asked for

Based on what CSRD-driven value-chain data collection tends to ask for, the requirements cluster into four groups. None of them are unusual for a factory that already answers quality questionnaires — they are simply asked in a different register.

### Climate and energy

- Energy consumption at the facility, by source, for the reporting period
- The split between electricity and fuel, and the split between grid and self-generated
- Scope 1 and Scope 2 emissions, and the method used to calculate them
- Fuel type for any on-site generation

This is the most common ask, because it is the easiest data to produce and the most standardised. A factory that already meters its electricity and fuel by source can answer this in an afternoon.

### Materials and traceability

- Fibre composition by percentage, by weight, across the product
- Where the fibre came from
- Certification status, with certificate numbers and issuing bodies, valid for the period

For cashmere specifically, the origin question is unusually well-defined. A Certificate of Origin naming the herder cooperative and the approximate GPS coordinates of the source pasture, with fibre traceable back to cooperative, harvest season and processing plant, is a stronger answer to a traceability question than most suppliers can give — because most suppliers cannot give one at all.

### Labour and working conditions

- Wages and the wage structure, and how they compare to the applicable legal benchmark
- Working hours, and the working-time arrangements
- Health and safety performance, and the incidence rate
- Whether the workforce is directly employed or contracted through a labour agency

The point worth making to a European buyer is about *why* this is asked. It is asked because the buying company cannot establish these conditions for itself and is required to describe them. It is a data request about your operation, not an audit with an agenda.

### Governance and business conduct

- Policies on bribery, corruption and competition law
- Incidents and how they were handled
- Anti-corruption controls and any third-party due diligence performed
- Whether the supplier has a code of conduct its own workers are subject to

## What a good response looks like

The suppliers who handle these questionnaires comfortably share a characteristic: **they have the data before they are asked for it.** The factory that maintains its own records can complete a value-chain data request in a day. The factory that has to reconstruct its answers will miss the deadline, and the buyer's default in that situation is to use an estimate — which weakens the brand's own disclosure and can create work for the supplier later.

Three things make the difference:

**1. Keep the raw records, not just the conclusions.** Meter readings by source, payroll by department, fuel delivery notes, fibre intake documentation with certificates attached, incident logs. The disclosures are compiled from these; they are not the same as them.

**2. Answer the question that was asked.** A questionnaire with forty fields wants forty fields. Substituting a general sustainability statement for a requested data point is the single most common reason a supplier's response is sent back.

**3. Say clearly what you do not have.** A factory that does not measure Scope 3 should say so and describe what it does measure. This is materially better than an estimate presented as a measurement — and it is what an ESRS-reporting company needs to know in order to treat your figures correctly.

## The strategic point for cashmere suppliers

There is a line of reasoning that runs through all of this, and it is worth stating plainly because it is commercially real.

The European brands that will be doing this reporting are the same brands competing for the same shelf space. Their reporting obligations are a *cost* for them, and a supplier who answers cleanly and quickly is, in a small but real way, cheaper to work with than a supplier who does not.

A traceability record, an energy record, a wage structure that can be stated — these are not audit exercises. They are the difference between being a supplier a European brand can report on, and one it has to estimate about.

The simplification of the CSRD does not change that. It changes how many buyers are obliged to ask, and how much they are asking for. It does not change the direction, and it does not change what a supplier with the data in hand is worth.

## What to do this quarter

A practical sequence, in the order it pays off:

1. **Check what you already hold.** Energy invoices and meter logs by source; fuel records; payroll structure; wage benchmark comparison; certificates with numbers and validity dates; fibre origin documentation; incident and grievance logs; anti-corruption policy. Much of what a value-chain request needs is probably already in a file somewhere.
2. **Close the obvious gaps.** If you have no payroll data broken down by department, or no fuel type recorded, that is a fixable gap and it is worth fixing while it is quiet.
3. **Write it down once.** A one-page supplier data sheet — energy mix, fibre origin, certification list, workforce structure, policies — that you can send in response to any request, updated annually.
4. **Keep the numbers with their method.** "2,140 MWh electricity, grid-supplied, 2025 calendar year, from metered consumption" is a usable answer. "Approximately 2,000 MWh" is not.

None of this is specific to CSRD, and none of it is unique to cashmere. That is precisely why it is worth doing: the supplier who has the records is the supplier who gets asked fewer follow-up questions by every European buyer, in every questionnaire that contains any sustainability element at all.
