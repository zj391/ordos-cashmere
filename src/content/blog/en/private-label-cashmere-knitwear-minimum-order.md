The MOQ on a private-label cashmere program is not a factory number. It is the sum of three separate minimums: the mill's batch minimum, the dyehouse's colorway minimum, and the trim minimum for labels and packaging.

A buyer asking "what is your MOQ" without specifying those three is asking the wrong question and will get an optimistic answer. This breaks down what actually sets each floor, what a first private-label order realistically costs across four product types, the lead-time components that are usually missing from a quotation, and the four ways buyers end up stuck with MOQ they cannot fill.

## Why the MOQ is not one number

**The mill's batch minimum** is set by what the machine can run economically. A combing line processing cashmere for 6 hours produces a certain weight; running it for 4 hours to make a smaller batch costs the same fixed overhead. Below roughly 30-50kg of yarn-equivalent, the mill is running a sub-scale batch.

**The dyehouse's colorway minimum** is set by the dye bath volume. A 300-liter bath needs a minimum fill weight or the liquor ratio is wrong and the shade is unrepeatable. In practice this is usually 8-15kg per colorway for piece dyeing, and it scales with the bath size the mill uses.

**The trim minimum** is set by the label and packaging supplier, not the mill. A woven-label supplier will not run 200 labels. The floor here is typically 500-1,000 units per trim item, which is why a small order can carry a surprisingly large trim cost per piece.

The three minimums are independent. A buyer can clear the mill minimum and still be unable to place the order because of a 1,000-label floor on a 400-piece order.

## What each product type realistically requires

| Product | Mill batch | Colorway | Trim | Practical first-order MOQ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cashmere scarf / wrap | 30-50kg yarn | 8-12kg per color | 500-1,000 labels | **150-300 pcs** |
| Cashmere knitwear (12GG) | 40-60kg yarn | 10-15kg per color | 500-1,000 labels + hangtags | **250-500 pcs** |
| Cashmere hat | 20-30kg yarn | 6-8kg per color | 500-1,000 labels | **300-600 pcs** |
| Cashmere blanket / throw | 60-100kg yarn | 15-25kg per color | 500 cartons minimum | **200-400 pcs** |

**These are floors, not targets.** A first order at the floor is a sampling order. A first order that actually launches a product is typically 2-4x the floor.

## What a first order actually costs

For a 300-piece cashmere knitwear private-label order, 4 colorways, at 15.5μm cashmere:

**Product cost**

- Yarn: 300 pieces × ~280g = 84kg of 15.5μm cashmere at 2/26 Nm. At a typical mill price for that grade, this is the dominant line item and it is roughly 55-65% of landed cost.
- Dyeing: 4 colorways × minimum bath fill. If the order is only 21kg per colorway, the effective per-kg dyeing cost is 30-50% higher than at 60kg.
- Knitting: 12GG full-fashioned or 5GG seamless — full-fashioned carries a setup cost per style that a 300-piece order amortizes badly.
- Trims: 300 pieces × (label + hangtag + packaging). If the label MOQ is 1,000, the per-piece label cost on 300 pieces can be 3-4x what it would be at 1,000 pieces.
- Sampling and PP sample: $400-1,200 depending on how many rounds.

**Order-level cost**

- First-order setup (patterns, tech pack translation, labels setup, packaging design): $1,500-4,000.
- Third-party inspection: $250-400 per inspector-day, 2-3 days on site for a first order.
- Freight (FOB Tianjin, DDP Europe for comparison): typically 6-9% of FOB value.

**What this means.** A 300-piece cashmere knitwear private-label order realistically runs $28,000-45,000 landed, of which the yarn is $17,000-27,000. The overheads — setup, trims amortized badly, sampling, inspection — add $4,000-8,000 that a 1,000-piece order spreads across four times as many units.

**This is the real reason to plan a second order.** The second order of the same product at 1,000 pieces typically costs 12-18% less per piece than the first.

## The lead-time components a quotation usually leaves out

A mill quotation that says "45 days" means production time. The actual elapsed time from deposit to delivery is:

| Component | Typical duration | Who controls it |
|---|---|---|
| Fiber relaxation before dyeing | 2-3 days | Mill |
| Dyeing + pre-shrink | 5-8 days | Mill |
| Knitting | 10-15 days | Mill |
| Washing / finishing | 3-5 days | Mill |
| Cut, sew, trim | 8-14 days | Mill or sub-contractor |
| Third-party inspection | 2-3 days | Buyer schedules |
| Sea freight Tianjin → Rotterdam | 30-38 days | Carrier |
| Customs + inland | 3-7 days | Broker |
| **Total, FOB** | **28-45 days** | |
| **Total, DDP European warehouse** | **65-90 days** | |

The two components buyers most often forget are **third-party inspection scheduling** (the mill needs 5 business days notice and the inspector needs a flight) and **sea freight**, which is longer than most people assume for the Tianjin-to-Europe lane — 30-38 days is normal, not exceptional.

## The four ways buyers get stuck

### 1. Hitting the mill minimum but not the trim minimum

The order clears the mill MOQ at 300 pieces but the label supplier will not run 300 labels. Result: either pay a 1,000-label minimum on a 300-piece order, or source the labels separately and lose the single-supplier accountability.

**Prevention:** ask for the trim MOQs in the same conversation as the mill MOQ.

### 2. Under-ordering on a multi-colorway program

Four colorways at 300 pieces total is 75 pieces per colorway, which is below the dyehouse minimum for most colorways. The mill either runs four sub-scale baths (shade variation between colorways) or quotes the order as three colorways at 100 pieces each.

**Prevention:** decide the color count before asking for the MOQ, and set the per-colorway minimum explicitly.

### 3. Ordering to the MOQ rather than to the demand

The most common commercial mistake. A buyer places 300 pieces because that is the MOQ, and then sells 180 in the first season. The remaining 120 is inventory at cost, in a fiber whose retail value drops every year.

**Prevention:** the MOQ should be the *upper bound* of what you can sell in two seasons, not the floor. If 300 is the MOQ and you can only commit 180, the answer is a different product or a different mill — not the same order with fewer pieces.

### 4. Not budgeting the first-order amortization

A 300-piece order at $38,000 looks expensive per piece. A 1,000-piece order of the same product at $96,000 looks 18% cheaper per piece. Buyers who compare quotations at different quantities make bad decisions.

**Prevention:** ask for a quantity-break table — 300 / 500 / 1,000 / 3,000 — and compare the *per-piece* figures at the quantity you will actually order.

## How to ask a mill for a real quotation

A request that gets a real answer:

> Product: 12GG full-fashioned crew-neck cashmere sweater, 100% cashmere 15.5μm.
> Quantity: 500 pieces, split across 3 colorways (170/170/160).
> Trims: woven label 1,000 pcs MOQ, printed hangtag 1,000 pcs MOQ, polybag 500 pcs MOQ.
> Please quote: 500 / 1,000 / 3,000 quantity breaks, per-piece FOB Tianjin.
> Include: yarn consumption per piece, dyeing minimum per colorway, setup charges, and total elapsed days from deposit to FOB.
> Target: first delivery within 90 days of deposit.

The specific ask that matters most is **elapsed days from deposit to FOB**, not production days. A mill quoting 45 production days and 60 elapsed days is being precise; a mill quoting 45 days and you find out it is 70 elapsed is not being honest.
