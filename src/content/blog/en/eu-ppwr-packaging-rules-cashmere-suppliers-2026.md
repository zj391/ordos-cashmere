# EU PPWR Is Live: What the Packaging Rules Mean for Cashmere Suppliers Shipping to Europe

Since **12 August 2026**, the European Union has been operating under a single, directly applicable packaging law: the **Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR), Regulation (EU) 2025/40**. It entered into force on 11 February 2025 and replaced the old Packaging Directive 94/62/EC, which was repealed on the same date the PPWR became applicable.

If you supply cashmere garments, scarves or hats into the European Union, this is not a distant policy question. It is now part of the ordinary commercial conversation between you and your European buyer, and the questions it raises reach back into the factory — into what goes in the polybag, what is printed on the hangtag, how the master carton is sealed, and what evidence you can produce when your buyer asks for it.

This is a working note on where the rules stand as of late October 2026, and what has concretely changed for an apparel supplier.

## The short version

- The PPWR is a **regulation**, not a directive. It applies directly and uniformly across all 27 member states, which removes the patchwork of 27 national transpositions that apparel exporters used to navigate.
- It covers **all packaging placed on the EU market regardless of material or origin**. A garment shipped from Inner Mongolia into Hamburg is in scope, exactly as a garment produced in Portugal is.
- Its scope is the **entire packaging life cycle**: design, substances, recycled content, minimisation, labelling, reuse, and end-of-life.
- The headline target is that **all packaging on the EU market must be recyclable in an economically viable way by 2030**.

## What actually changed for an apparel supplier

The regulation is not a single obligation. It is a stack of them, and the ones that reach a knitwear factory are worth separating out.

### 1. Minimisation, including empty space

PPWR carries forward and strengthens the minimisation logic that already existed under the Directive. The practical test is whether the packaging does the job the package is there to do — protection, containment, information — without adding material for no reason.

For apparel this lands in a few specific places:

- **Polybag and header bags.** A sweater shipped in a bag sized for a coat is excess packaging. Buyers increasingly ask for the bag to be dimensioned to the garment.
- **Master cartons.** Over-sized cartons do not survive the 2030 recyclability logic, and they are the easiest thing for a buyer to spot in a packing audit.
- **Void fill.** Paper and plastic void fill are both now measured against the same "is it necessary" test.

This is not an abstract eco-design exercise. It is a cost conversation, because a buyer who finds your packaging is not minimised will ask for a quote that reflects the redesign — and usually negotiate it downward.

### 2. Labelling and material identification

Packaging placed on the EU market has to carry information identifying the material, in a way the EU's own identification system recognises. For a textile supplier this generally means the packaging for a garment has to declare its constituent materials in the prescribed format, and the packaging supplier has to be able to evidence that the declaration matches the material actually used.

The practical consequence: **your packaging supplier's data becomes your evidence.** You need their declaration, and you need to keep it, because the question of whether the label is correct is a question about your shipment, not theirs.

### 3. Substances of concern

PPWR restricts substances of concern in packaging, including restrictions on **PFAS in food-contact packaging** above defined thresholds. PFAS in apparel packaging is not the same regulatory question as PFAS in food contact, but it is the direction the framework is moving, and it shows up in buyer questionnaires as a substances-declaration request.

If your packaging contains a fluorinated treatment for oil or water resistance — some polybags and some barrier papers do — that is worth being able to answer about proactively.

### 4. Recyclability and recycled content

The 2030 objective is the driver behind the recyclability and recycled-content provisions. The direction of travel for every packaging category is the same: **recycled content targets, design-for-recycling rules, and format requirements** that will keep tightening through the decade.

For an apparel supplier the practical question is usually narrower and more immediate: *is my packaging design one that the buyer's recycling stream can actually handle?* Film, paper and rigid plastics do not travel the same route, and mixed-material laminations are the hardest case of all.

### 5. The authorised representative question — still open

One point deserves flagging because it is genuinely unresolved. Under the framework that applies to producers placing packaged goods on the market, a producer established outside the EU selling directly into member states has in principle been expected to appoint an **authorised representative**.

In December 2025 the European Commission proposed, as part of the so-called **Environmental Omnibus** package, to **suspend** that obligation for producers selling packaged products directly to consumers in member states other than the one where they are located. **That proposal is still before the European Parliament and the Council as of this writing.** It has not become law.

For a B2B cashmere supplier the practical read is: a business-to-business sale to a retailer or distributor is not a sale to consumers, so the direct-to-consumer framing of the proposal is not the situation most apparel exporters are in. But if you sell to end consumers in the EU directly — through your own storefront, for instance — the question is live and the answer is genuinely uncertain right now. If that describes part of your business, your counsel should be tracking it rather than relying on a summary.

## What to have ready before your next European order

This is the part worth acting on. Four documents, or four answers, that cost very little to prepare and save a week when an EU buyer asks.

**1. A packaging specification per product.** What goes in the bag, what the bag is made of, what the hangtag is made of, what the carton is made of, and what the void fill is. Per SKU, not generic. Write it down once and reuse it.

**2. A packaging weight record per shipment.** Buyers moving to PPWR are asking for packaging weight against product weight, because it is the only comparable ratio they have. If you can produce grams of packaging per garment, you can produce grams of packaging per garment.

**3. Your supplier's material declarations.** The labelling obligations sit with the packaging, but the record sits with whoever puts the packaged product on the market. Ask your polybag and carton suppliers for their material declarations in writing and file them.

**4. A substances answer for any fluorinated treatment.** If you use it, say so and provide what your supplier states. If you do not, say that too. Both are better than a delay while someone finds out.

## What has not changed

It is worth saying plainly what has *not* shifted, because a lot of the conversation around this regulation is noise:

- **Your fibre, yarn and garment certifications are untouched.** OEKO-TEX, ISO 9001, BSCI and GCS certification of the product itself are a separate matter from packaging. A supplier who conflates the two is either confused or selling something.
- **No new MOQ, no new lead time.** PPWR constrains the packaging, not the manufacturing.
- **No labelling requirement on the garment itself.** This is packaging regulation. The textile labelling rules are a different instrument and they have their own timeline.
- **No requirement to source European packaging.** The rules govern what you place on the market, not where you buy the packaging from.

## How this shows up in a European buyer conversation

In our experience, the first time PPWR comes up with a European buyer it tends to arrive as a **question rather than a demand**, usually from someone in sustainability or supply chain rather than procurement. It usually looks like one of these:

- "Can you confirm what our garments will be packed in and the weight of that packaging per unit?"
- "Do your packaging suppliers provide material declarations we can file?"
- "Can you reduce the bag size on the sweater line?"
- "Is anything in your packaging fluorinated?"

All four are answerable on the same day, if the packaging data exists in a form you can hand over. That is the entire preparation.

## Where this goes next

Two things are worth watching over the next twelve months.

First, the **implementing acts under the PPWR** — the delegated and implementing acts that set out the detailed recyclability methodologies, the design-for-recycling rules and the calculation methods for recycled content — arrive on a rolling basis after application. These will turn the framework's directional targets into specific rules a packaging designer has to satisfy.

Second, the **Environmental Omnibus proposal** on the authorised representative obligation moves through Parliament and Council. Its outcome changes the position for direct-to-consumer sellers and leaves everyone else where it is.

If your packaging data is not currently in a form you can hand to a buyer in a day, that is the one thing on this list worth fixing before the next order rather than after it.
