The most expensive lesson in cashmere sourcing in 2026 is the same one buyers have been learning for the last decade: a cashmere sweater at a unit price that is 30% below comparable offers is rarely a bargain. It is usually a supply chain problem you cannot see from the price quote — wrong fiber, wrong origin, wrong dehairing, wrong yarn count, or all four. The fix is a structured supply chain evaluation before the first PO, not a complaint letter after the third rejection.

This page is the supply chain evaluation checklist we walk through with new wholesale clients during their first site visit. It is what an experienced B2B cashmere buyer's QC team would test, in the order they would test it, with the documentation they would request. None of it is proprietary — every item is a known industry standard. The difference is doing all of it, in the right order, with the right person on the supplier side answering.

## 1. The four supply chain risk vectors in cashmere

The cashmere supply chain has four points where quality, cost, and risk diverge. Every cashmere B2B program gets evaluated on the same four vectors, in this order:

**Vector 1: Herd and fiber origin.** Where is the cashmere fiber physically coming from, who owns the goats, who tends them, who shears them, who buys the raw fiber from the herders, and what is the traceback path from your finished garment back to the goat? This is the UFLPA / CBAM / Lacey Act question. The answer is verifiable — it is a paper trail.

**Vector 2: Dehairing and processing controls.** Dehairing is the most expensive step in the fiber preparation (roughly 30-40% of the total cost of a 100% cashmere finished garment), and the step that introduces the most variability in hand, micron consistency, and contamination level. A mill can be honest about its herd sourcing and still produce inconsistent yarn if its dehairing is sloppy. This is the process question.

**Vector 3: Yarn count and gauge honesty.** Yarn count (Nm 2/26, 2/48, 2/60) and stitch density (7gg, 12gg, 14gg) are two of the most-often-misrepresented specs in cashmere contracts. A 12gg garment that is actually 9gg will pass visual inspection at the sample room but fail on every repeat order. This is the spec question.

**Vector 4: Margin structure and risk-sharing.** A mill that quotes at 6% margin on a fixed-quantity PO is a stable business partner. A mill that quotes at 2% margin is one bad PO away from bankruptcy. This is the business question.

Most B2B buyers focus on Vector 1 because that is what the regulation and media attention has put in front of them. The four are not equal — Vector 1 is necessary but not sufficient. A buyer who only audits Vector 1 will get a clean supply chain and a yarn quality problem; a buyer who only audits Vector 3 will get a great-looking first sample and a quality disaster on the second repeat.

## 2. Vector 1: The 8-document traceback checklist

For Vector 1, you need a paper trail that ties your finished garment back to the herder. We provide the following 8 documents for every PO above $5,000. We give them to wholesale clients, brand buyers, and customs brokers on request. Most reputable Ordos mills can produce the same set; if your supplier cannot, that is a yellow flag, not a red flag — but a yellow flag is enough to slow down the relationship.

1. **Certificate of Origin** issued by the local Industry & Commerce Bureau (鄂尔多斯市工商行政管理局) with the Bureau stamp. This is the single most important document for customs. It states the location of dehairing, spinning, and finishing. Not a self-issued factory certificate.
2. **Processing flow declaration** with each manufacturing step and the geographic location where it occurs. UFLPA enforcement specifically looks for this — it should include the dehairing line, the spinning line, the knitting/weaving line, and the finishing line, with addresses.
3. **Traceback summary** for the fiber lots in your shipment. We identify the herder cooperatives (合作社) that supplied the raw cashmere, with their approximate locations and the season of harvest. Inner Mongolia outside of Xinjiang = no UFLPA issue, but CBP wants the paperwork anyway.
4. **Electricity source declaration** for CBAM. Inner Mongolia grid is approximately 65% coal / 35% wind and solar. We provide the Chinese grid emission factor and the verifiable kWh consumption per kg of yarn output. This determines the CBAM certificate cost.
5. **Fur-fiber absence declaration** for shipments to the EU and California. Confirms that no angora or rabbit fiber is blended into the lot. Routine CBP question on small cashmere lots.
6. **OEKO-TEX Standard 100 certificate** for our yarns. Downloadable from the buyer dashboard. Reduces chemical-input questions under UFLPA.
7. **2025 Sedex SMETA 4-pillar audit report** on file. Available on request for buyers above $50K PO volume.
8. **ISO 9001:2015 + BSCI audit certificates**. Also available on request.

If your supplier can produce all 8 within 5 business days of PO confirmation, the documentation is in good shape. If they need 3-4 weeks, the supply chain is not actually traceable in the way the documentation suggests.

## 3. Vector 2: The 3-test processing audit

For Vector 2, the audit is hands-on, not paper-based. We suggest 3 tests that any reputable mill will let you run during a site visit. If they will not, the dehairing has problems they know about.

**Test 1: Micron consistency test.** Take 50g of yarn from 3 different cones of the same lot. Send to an ISO 17025 lab for laser-micron testing. If the average micron is within 0.5µm of the spec and the standard deviation is under 0.4µm, the dehairing is consistent. If the standard deviation is over 0.6µm, the dehairing is mixing fiber grades. The lab cost is roughly $150-300 per sample; we run this in-house before every bulk production release.

**Test 2: Hand-feel test by a qualified grader.** This is the test that experienced cashmere buyers rely on most. A trained grader can tell in 30 seconds whether a yarn is 100% cashmere, a blend, or a sub-grade fiber. The grader does not need a lab — they need 10+ years of working with cashmere yarn daily. Most brand buyers do not have this expertise in-house; if you do not, hire a third-party consultant for the first 2-3 site visits.

**Test 3: Combing pass count.** Dehairing is a multi-pass operation. Cashmere fiber needs 2-3 combing passes to remove the coarse guard hair and the residual lanolin. A single-pass dehairing is what gives cashmere its "itchy" reputation. Ask the mill to walk you through the dehairing line; count the combing stations; confirm the fiber makes 2 or 3 passes. Single-pass is a red flag.

These three tests are not exhaustive — the full site visit checklist is closer to 20 items — but they are the three that catch 80% of the actual problems we see in cashmere.

## 4. Vector 3: The yarn-count verification

For Vector 3, the test is the most objective and the easiest to do. A 2/48 Nm yarn has 48 kilometers of yarn per kilogram of fiber. A 2/26 Nm yarn has 26. If your supplier says they delivered 2/48 but the actual yarn is 2/36, you can prove it in 3 minutes with a wrap reel and a precision scale.

The procedure:

1. Wrap the yarn on a 1-meter wrap reel. Make 100 wraps (= 100 meters).
2. Weigh the 100-meter skein on a precision scale (0.1g resolution).
3. The weight in grams × 10 = the yarn count in Nm (e.g. 4.17g × 10 = 41.7m/g, which means 41.7 kilometers per kilogram, which is 2/41.7 Nm — close to 2/42, not 2/48).
4. Repeat on 5 skeins from 5 different cones of the same lot.
5. If the average count is within 5% of the spec, the yarn is honest. If it is off by 10% or more, the yarn count is wrong.

Buyers who do this test on every first PO from a new mill catch roughly 1 in 4 yarn-count misrepresentations. The misrepresentations are not always intentional — sometimes they are a sourcing-team error or a communication gap between the spinning mill and the finishing mill. But the practical effect on the buyer is the same: a wrong-count yarn produces a wrong-gauge garment.

## 5. Vector 4: The margin conversation

Vector 4 is uncomfortable but necessary. A mill that quotes at 2% margin on a $50,000 fixed-quantity PO has $1,000 of margin to absorb a 3% input cost spike (which is normal in cashmere — micron shortages, currency moves, transport rate changes). A mill that quotes at 6% margin has $3,000 of buffer for the same spike. The difference between a stable supplier and a flaky one is mostly this.

The honest questions to ask:

- What is your margin on this PO? (A good mill will answer this directly. A bad mill will dodge.)
- What is your worst-case annual raw fiber cost volatility, and how much of that do you absorb before renegotiating? (For cashmere, a 20% raw fiber cost swing in a single season is normal.)
- What is your credit line for raw fiber purchases? (The answer indicates the mill's working capital, which is a leading indicator of stability.)
- What is your annual dehairing capacity utilization, and what is the saturation point? (If the mill is at 90% utilization, they have no buffer for emergency orders — and no buffer for your first repeat order either.)

These are uncomfortable questions. The mills that answer them are the ones to buy from. The mills that refuse to answer, or that get visibly uncomfortable, are the ones that will have supply problems at the worst possible moment for you.

## 6. The 6 red flags to walk away from

If any of these 6 come up in the first 2-3 conversations, walk away. The cashmere market in 2026 is competitive enough that there is no reason to take on a supplier with structural risk.

**Red flag 1: "We can do it cheaper if you don't need the OEKO-TEX certificate."** A mill that suggests this is openly telling you that they have a parallel production line that is not OEKO-TEX compliant. The cheaper line is the one you will get.

**Red flag 2: "Our herder cooperative is in Xinjiang."** The first question every B2B buyer should ask. The first answer every B2B mill should give honestly. If the answer is Xinjiang, walk away — the UFLPA exposure is total and your customs broker will not be able to clear the shipment.

**Red flag 3: "We don't need a contract — we can do this on trust."** A mill that wants to skip the contract wants to skip the consequences if quality is bad. A written contract is the only protection B2B buyers have against silent downgrade.

**Red flag 4: "Our minimum order is 30 pcs."** For 100% cashmere, MOQ is constrained by dehairing batch size, which is 50-200kg of fiber. A 30-pc MOQ means the mill is either blending your order into someone else's batch (and you will not get pure cashmere) or they are holding small inventory (and the fiber is old).

**Red flag 5: "We can ship next week."** Cashmere yarn production is 14-21 days for a standard 2/26 Nm at 100kg. Cashmere knitwear is 30-45 days. A "next week" promise means the mill is shipping from inventory, not from a fresh production run, and you do not know what is in the inventory.

**Red flag 6: "We don't have a fixed price list, every order is quoted."** A mill that re-quotes every order is one whose costs are unstable — usually a sign that the mill is buying fiber on spot markets and passing the cost volatility to the buyer. A reputable mill has a quarterly pricing model with 30-60 day forward visibility.

## 7. The first 90 days: a recommended sequence

For a new B2B cashmere relationship, the first 90 days should look like this:

**Days 1-14: Document review.** Get the 8 documents in section 2. Read them. Verify the 6 red flags in section 6. If the mill can produce the documents and clear the red flags, proceed.

**Days 15-30: Sample room visit.** Visit the mill in person. Run the 3 tests in section 3. Ask the 4 questions in section 5. Take your own photos of the dehairing line, the spinning line, the knitting line, the finishing line. Save them for future reference.

**Days 30-60: First PO.** Place a small first PO (we recommend $5,000-15,000 minimum to get meaningful data on repeat performance). The first PO is a learning PO, not a profit PO. The goal is to validate the mill can deliver what they promised.

**Days 60-90: Yarn-count verification and quality audit.** Run the yarn-count verification (section 4) on the delivered goods. Run a hand-feel evaluation. If the first PO passes, place a second PO at 2-3x the size. If the second PO also passes, you have a supplier.

**Months 4-12: Scale.** Once the relationship is established, the typical ramp pattern for a B2B cashmere relationship is 3-5x year-over-year for the first 3 years, then 1.5-2x for years 4-5. This is the typical B2B cycle for an apparel material supplier.
