# Ordos vs Nepal vs Tibet Cashmere: B2B Quality, Cost, and Lead Time Compared

A B2B buyer asking "where should I source cashmere from" usually gets one of three answers — Ordos (Inner Mongolia), Nepal, or Tibet. Each region produces cashmere commercially, and each has built a distinct reputation: Ordos for bulk-supply reliability and price competitiveness, Nepal for the finest micron and the pashmina tradition, Tibet for high-altitude fiber length. The reality underneath those reputations is more nuanced, and the right choice depends on which of three things you are prioritizing — fiber quality, cost per kilo, or volume flexibility.

This page compares the three regions on the variables a sourcing decision actually depends on: micron range and length (the two fiber parameters that determine quality), color and dye behavior, yield from raw fiber, price per kilo, MOQ and lead time, and end-use suitability. The goal is to give a buyer the framework to pick the right origin for their specific product, not to declare one region universally better.

## 1. Fiber quality: micron and length

Cashmere fiber quality is determined primarily by two parameters — fiber diameter (measured in microns) and fiber length (measured in millimeters). The grade (A/B/C) is a function of these two numbers.

| Region | Typical micron range | Typical length range | Grade mix (A/B/C share) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Ordos (Inner Mongolia)** | 14.5-19.5 µm | 28-36 mm | 25% / 50% / 25% | Best bulk consistency, broadest grade coverage |
| **Alashan (Inner Mongolia)** | 14.5-17.0 µm | 32-40 mm | 45% / 40% / 15% | Premium inner-Mongolia grade, comparable to Mongolian goat |
| **Nepal (high-altitude pashmina)** | 12.5-16.0 µm | 30-50 mm | 50% / 40% / 10% | Finest micron globally, but lower yield |
| **Tibet (high-altitude)** | 14.0-17.5 µm | 32-42 mm | 35% / 50% / 15% | Longer fiber length, premium grade share |

The most important point: Nepal's reputation for being the 'finest' cashmere in the world is accurate — the 12.5-14.5 micron fiber from Chyangra goats (the Nepalese mountain goat) is genuinely finer than anything produced at scale in Inner Mongolia. But micron is only one parameter, and the trade-off is yield. Nepalese Chyangra goats produce roughly 100-150 grams of usable dehaired fiber per animal per year, compared to 250-400 grams for Inner Mongolian Alashan goats. The lower yield is the main reason Nepalese cashmere commands a 40-80% higher price per kilo than Ordos cashmere of comparable grade.

For B2B buyers, the practical implication is that Nepalese cashmere is the right choice when fiber fineness is the primary product differentiator (luxury scarves, premium yarn for 14+ gauge knitwear, soft-handle accessories). For most other cashmere product categories — knitwear at 7-12 gauge, woven fabric, mid-range accessories — Inner Mongolian cashmere at the equivalent grade gives the same end-product hand-feel at a 40-80% lower fiber cost.

## 2. Color and dye behavior

Cashmere fiber color is partly genetic (the goat breed) and partly environmental (the grazing conditions). All three regions produce the three natural cashmere colors — white, brown (called 'qing' in Inner Mongolia), and purple/grey (called 'zi') — but the proportions differ.

| Region | White fiber share | Brown fiber share | Purple/grey fiber share |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ordos** | 75-80% | 15-20% | 5-8% |
| **Alashan** | 70-75% | 18-22% | 5-8% |
| **Nepal** | 35-45% | 40-50% | 10-15% |
| **Tibet** | 50-60% | 30-35% | 8-12% |

White cashmere is the most dyeable (can be bleached to off-white and dyed to any color), so Inner Mongolian white cashmere is the default fiber for Pantone-matched custom dye programs. Nepalese and Tibetan white fiber is rarer and commands a premium. Brown and purple fiber is typically used undyed — over-dyeing brown or purple to a saturated color requires an extra bleaching step that weakens the fiber, so most brown/purple cashmere is sold as undyed natural color.

## 3. Yield from raw fiber

Raw cashmere fiber (the combed fiber harvested from the goat) contains guard hair, dander, and vegetable matter. The dehairing process — separating the soft underdown from the coarse guard hair — typically yields 25-40% of usable cashmere fiber from raw fiber by weight. The yield depends on the dehairing line and the source herd.

| Region | Typical dehairing yield | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Ordos** | 30-40% | Industrial dehairing lines, consistent yield |
| **Alashan** | 28-35% | Slightly lower yield, higher grade share |
| **Nepal** | 18-28% | Hand-combing tradition, lower yield |
| **Tibet** | 22-30% | Mixed mechanical and hand-combing |

The yield matters because the price per kilo of dehaired cashmere is calculated from the price of the raw fiber. If raw Ordos cashmere costs $60/kg and the yield is 35%, the dehaired cashmere cost is $60 / 0.35 = $171/kg before further processing. If raw Nepalese cashmere costs $120/kg and the yield is 22%, the dehaired cashmere cost is $120 / 0.22 = $545/kg. The fiber cost difference of $374/kg is then passed through the yarn spinning, dyeing, and garment stages.

## 4. Price per kilo and MOQ

The price per kilo varies by grade, region, and processing stage. The 2026 approximate ranges for dehaired cashmere top (undyed, scoured, ready for spinning) are:

| Origin | Grade A (14.5-15.5 µm) | Grade A/B (15.5-16.5 µm) | Grade B (16.5-17.5 µm) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ordos** | $50-65/kg | $42-55/kg | $32-42/kg |
| **Alashan** | $65-85/kg | $55-70/kg | $42-55/kg |
| **Nepal (Chyangra)** | $120-180/kg | $85-120/kg | $65-85/kg |
| **Tibet** | $75-100/kg | $60-80/kg | $48-65/kg |

For finished yarn, add the spinning cost (roughly $10-25/kg depending on yarn count, with finer counts costing more). For finished knitwear, add the knitting, dyeing, and finishing cost (which is typically the same regardless of fiber origin).

MOQ is similar across regions at the finished yarn and garment stage (typically 30-50kg per yarn lot, 50-100 finished pieces per style). At the raw fiber stage, Ordos and Alashan suppliers typically accept 10-50kg MOQ per grade, while Nepal and Tibet suppliers typically require 50-100kg minimum because the fiber is processed in smaller lots.

## 5. Lead time and supply reliability

Lead time for cashmere fiber supply varies by region and by season:

- **Ordos (Inner Mongolia)**: 30-45 day lead time for dehaired top from order confirmation, 60-90 days for finished yarn, 90-120 days for finished garments. Industrial-scale supply, year-round availability with peak pricing in March-May (comb season) and lower pricing in October-December (post-harvest inventory).
- **Nepal**: 60-90 day lead time for dehaired top, 90-120 days for yarn. Smaller-scale supply, with annual yield variations. More vulnerable to monsoon season logistics disruptions (June-August).
- **Tibet**: 60-90 day lead time for dehaired top, 90-120 days for yarn. Mid-scale supply, more reliable than Nepal but less than Ordos. Logistics transit time through inland China adds 10-15 days versus Ordos coastal shipments.

Supply reliability — the probability that a confirmed order can be filled at the agreed price and timeline — is materially different across regions. Ordos is the most reliable at industrial volumes (we are a Tier-1 supplier at our mill in Ordos with consistent capacity). Nepal is reliable at small-batch volumes (50-200kg per order) but less reliable at bulk volumes (>500kg). Tibet is mid-range. The reliability difference is one of the main reasons Ordos has become the dominant cashmere supplier for B2B buyers scaling up to 10,000+ piece programs.

## 6. Which origin for which product

A simplified mapping of cashmere origin to product category:

- **Luxury scarves and shawls (pashmina tradition)**: Nepal. The 12.5-14.5 micron fiber is the genuine 'pashmina' standard, and the lower yield and higher cost are appropriate for the product positioning.
- **Premium yarn for 14+ gauge knitwear**: Alashan or Nepal, depending on price tolerance. Alashan is the better value when the buyer wants premium fiber without the full Nepal premium.
- **Mid-to-premium knitwear (7-12 gauge)**: Ordos Grade A or A/B. The micron range (15.5-16.5) is the sweet spot for 7-12 gauge knitwear, and the cost is roughly half of Nepal at equivalent grade.
- **Heavy-gauge knitwear (3-7 gauge)**: Ordos Grade B. Cost-driven, the hand-feel difference between Grade A and Grade B at 5-7 gauge is small but the cost difference is meaningful.
- **Woven fabric (overcoating, suiting)**: Ordos or Alashan, with the grade chosen based on the warp/weft yarn count.
- **Cashmere accessories (gloves, hats, socks)**: Ordos Grade B or A/B. The product category does not require the very finest micron, so the cost saving is appropriate.
- **Undyed natural-color cashmere**: All three origins. Nepal and Tibet produce more brown and purple fiber, which can be a positioning advantage for the natural-color trend.
