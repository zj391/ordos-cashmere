**Chinese New Year falls on Saturday 6 February 2027.** In Inner Mongolia — where most of the world's fine cashmere is combed, spun, knitted and finished — that single date governs a shutdown window that typically runs for two to three weeks on either side of it.

If you are placing or planning AW26/27 replenishment now, or booking SS27 production in the next quarter, the February date is not a footnote on your calendar. It is a structural constraint on when goods can physically exist.

This is a planning note, written on 30 October 2026, about how the 2027 window falls and what it means for orders in flight.

## The date, and why it matters more than it looks

New Year's Day follows the lunar calendar. CNY 2027 lands on **6 February 2027, a Saturday**, which means the public holiday period straddles the weekend. The practical consequence is that the working week before it is short, and the week after it is consumed by people travelling home and back.

A factory running to its own schedule handles this in roughly three layers:

| Layer | Typical window | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Slow-down | Late January | Output drops as workers begin travelling. Knitting lines run fewer shifts; finishing and packing slow first because they are the last stages. |
| Shutdown | Early to mid February | Production effectively stops. The exact span varies year to year and by how much work is already in the pipe. |
| Restart | Late February | Workers return, output ramps back over one to two weeks, and the pipeline behind it refills. |

Three things follow from that, and they are the three that actually move a delivery date.

**First, the last week of January is not a normal week.** If a style has to be knitted, finished and packed to catch a sailing, the realistic cut-off is earlier than the calendar suggests — often well before the nominal shutdown date.

**Second, the shutdown is not fully dead time.** Inventory already knitted and packed before the break ships normally. What stops is *new* production. A factory with three weeks of finished stock will deliver through the period; one with nothing in the pipe will not.

**Third, the restart has a ramp.** The first week back is not a full week. Output climbs back over roughly ten days, which pushes anything that missed January out by a corresponding amount.

## What this means for the orders you have in flight

### AW26/27 replenishment — the urgent one

Right now, in late October 2026, AW26/27 is the season being replenished, and it is the season most exposed to the February gap. The reasoning is straightforward: a garment that has to reach European shelves for the January sales period has to be finished and shipped before the slow-down, and a garment restocked in February or March is competing for a season that is already half over.

Two practical consequences.

**If you need AW26/27 delivery in January 2027, the working-backwards date is already past for anything requiring new production.** Late October is inside the window where the order needs to be in the knitting queue, not in the discussion stage. This is the point at which a factory's honest answer to "can you deliver by January?" starts with "for which styles, and from which stock position".

**If you need AW26/27 delivery in February or March 2027, plan for a March landing, not a February one.** The month either side of CNY carries roughly a week of lost output, and the ramp adds more. A delivery quoted as "late February" across the shutdown is almost always optimistic; March is the realistic month.

### SS27 pre-orders — the comfortable one

SS27 production for European delivery is typically in the factory during spring, which is exactly the window the shutdown occupies. The trade is straightforward: **SS27 pre-orders placed early secure their place in the queue before the break; orders placed in December compete for a queue restarting in late February.**

The commercial read: the SS27 pre-order window is shorter than it looks, and the buyers who lock production and colour before the break are the ones who get their SS27 delivery on the date they wanted. The buyers who wait until January are the ones who accept April.

### Yarn and raw material — a different risk profile

Yarn is a different calendar from finished knitwear. Spinning runs continuously, and the key question is not whether the mill stops but whether the **delivery schedule before the break leaves enough time for knitting, finishing and packing** to happen before the shutdown rather than during it.

The practical rule: **the last yarn delivery that can be knitted into shippable goods before the break needs to land around early January**, not late January. Yarn arriving in the second half of January becomes February production, which becomes March delivery.

## The five questions worth asking a supplier right now

These are the questions that actually change a delivery date, and they are the ones worth putting in writing before the order rather than after the invoice.

1. **"Which of our styles will be in finished-goods stock before the January slow-down, and which require production after it?"** This one question separates an order that will arrive on time from one that will not, and it is rarely asked early enough to be useful.

2. **"What is your production cut-off date for AW26/27 delivery in January, counting back from the CNY shutdown?"** A factory that answers with a specific date is a factory that has planned the shutdown. One that answers "end of January" has not.

3. **"What is the realistic restart date, and how many weeks of output does that cost?"** The honest answer is usually "around 10 days of ramp", and it should be part of the quoted lead time rather than discovered in March.

4. **"Is there finished stock of this style already in your warehouse?"** If yes, the shutdown is irrelevant to that style. If no, it is the whole story.

5. **"How does the CNY period affect the shipping schedule — sailing frequency, and the transshipment through Chinese ports over the holiday?"** Chinese ports operate through the holiday, but vessel space, consolidation schedules and the pre-holiday rush are all different from a normal week, and a factory that only controls production does not control this. Ask who books the freight and what happens to the booking.

## Where the pressure actually shows up

From the factory side, three things create the congestion, and it is worth understanding them because they explain why a supplier may be conservative about a January date:

**Capacity is already committed.** A cashmere factory running knitting lines through the AW season is running them for other buyers too. A slot for your style in January is a slot taken from somewhere else.

**The finishing bottleneck is before the knitting bottleneck.** Most factories have more knitting capacity than finishing capacity. Knitting is elastic — you can add a shift, or run a style on a different line. Finishing — washing, blocking, ironing, final inspection, folding, packing — is a fixed line with fixed people. The congestion builds there.

**Late-October is when the Q1 schedule gets allocated.** Most factories allocate January and February capacity in the same planning cycle as the rest of the year, and that allocation is informed by orders in hand. The moment to influence the Q1 plan is now, in the form of a confirmed order. A forecast does not hold a slot.

## A note on the year of the goat

CNY 2027 opens the **Year of the Goat (Wei Wei)** in the Chinese zodiac, beginning on 6 February 2027 and running to 26 January 2028. For a factory in Inner Mongolia this is the shortest of the twelve holiday periods — the Goat year begins with the new moon closest to 4 February, which is why the date falls early in February rather than in late January as it did in 2025.

The practical difference from a late-January CNY is that **the slow-down starts later and the total window is shorter**. A factory does not get an extra week of reduced output; it gets the same lost weeks compressed more tightly around a holiday that many people use to travel earlier. If you are planning, plan on the same two-to-three-week shutdown, and treat late January as already reduced.

## What to do in the next two weeks

This is the part with a deadline in it.

1. **List the styles you need delivered before the shutdown** — those for January European delivery, and any SS27 style you want to start in the spring. This is the list that needs production slots.

2. **Ask for stock positions.** For each style on that list: how many pieces are already knitted, how many are in finishing, how many are packed. The answer tells you whether you are buying a slot or buying stock.

3. **Confirm the January production cut-off in writing, per style.** Not per order — per style, because styles compete for different lines and a factory's honest answer is usually a mix.

4. **Lock SS27 now if the colours and quantities are decided.** If they are not decided, decide the colour and the quantity in November rather than in January. The factory's Q1 allocation happens in November and December, and a confirmed spec holds a slot.

5. **Book freight early for anything shipping in December and January.** Vessel space and consolidation into the pre-holiday period is a separate constraint from production, and it tightens before the factory does.

None of this is exotic. It is the ordinary consequence of a lunar calendar meeting a supply chain that runs on solar months. The suppliers who handle it well are simply the ones who treat the February date as a fixed constraint in the schedule rather than an interruption to be absorbed later.
